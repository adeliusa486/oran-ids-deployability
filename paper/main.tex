\begin{abstract}
Intrusion detectors for 5G and O-RAN traffic are often reported above 99\% accuracy on a random split of one corpus. We test what such a figure predicts about deployment on NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD and 5G-NIDD, with six architectures, two input-blind baselines and a ladder of protocols. A random split raises radio-layer macro-$F_1$ by \LeakAvg{} (95\% interval \LeakAvgNbLo{} to \LeakAvgNbHi{}; significant for two sequence models), and a nearest-neighbor lookup gains \NNGain{}: the models recognize capture sessions. Because the radio capture is ordered by scenario, a time-disjoint split holds out attack categories; with coverage fixed, time order is indistinguishable from session variation, and a held-out benign session draws a false positive rate between 0.00 and \BenHighMax{}. In 5G-NIDD, \LcBenignTwinPct\% of benign flows copy flood records labeled as attacks elsewhere, which caps balanced accuracy at \LcCeilNative{}. The dataset authors' 99.9\% accuracy reproduces but rests on two record-position fields that separate the conflicting records; without them it falls to \PubOneNoposAccMax\%. Transferred to 5G-NIDD flows with consistent labels, detectors reach balanced accuracy \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}, below the target's own reference, and none passes a deployability test that the published pipeline passes on its own split. A third corpus shows the same pattern. At a prevalence of 0.002, operational precision reaches at most \NetCleanPPVbest{} at any threshold that keeps recall above 10\%, and ten benign radio sessions cannot bound the radio alert burden. Averaging precision over folds inflates the best-to-worst ratio between architectures from \EstPooled{} to \EstMean{}. We close with reporting requirements for deployability claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) architectures disaggregate the base station into radio, distributed and central units connected by open interfaces, and add a RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) that hosts third-party control applications~\cite{polese2023understanding,abdalla2022toward,niknam2022intelligent}. The near-real-time RIC runs close to the cells it controls~\cite{shi2016edge,bonati2021intelligence}, so it observes the telemetry of every device those cells serve. Compromised devices, many of them IoT devices, are a documented source of volumetric attacks~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai,kolias2017ddos}. A detection application (xApp) inside the RIC control loop is therefore an attractive defense~\cite{polese2023coloran,schmidt2021flexric,wen2024spector,scalingi2024detran}.

Detectors for 5G and O-RAN traffic are usually evaluated on a random held-out split of one dataset, and the reported accuracy or $F_1$ is commonly above 99\%. On 5G-NIDD, one of the two corpora we use, the dataset's authors report binary accuracy of 99.85\% to 99.95\% for four of five classifiers~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv}, and later work reports weighted $F_1$ of up to 99.95\%~\cite{ilias2024moe5g}. Three properties decide whether such a detector is deployable, and a held-out split of the training distribution measures none of them. The first is generalization to a deployment that did not supply the training data; intrusion detectors are known to transfer poorly between independently collected datasets~\cite{layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. The second is alert burden: a false positive rate that looks small on a balanced benchmark becomes a large alert volume once it is multiplied by production flow rates and read against a realistic attack base rate~\cite{axelsson2000baserate,sommer2010outside}. The third is latency against the near-real-time control loop, which the O-RAN specification places between 10\,ms and 1\,s~\cite{oran_wg3_ricarch}.

Each of these critiques has been made before. The base-rate argument is Axelsson's~\cite{axelsson2000baserate}, the closed-world critique is Sommer and Paxson's~\cite{sommer2010outside}, and evaluation pitfalls in security machine learning have been catalogued by Arp \emph{et al.}~\cite{arp2022dosdonts}, with temporal and spatial bias formalized by Pendlebury \emph{et al.}~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}. Transfer between the two corpora we use has been measured by Abraheem and Edhirig~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}, and inference inside a real near-real-time RIC has been measured by Obiuwevwi \emph{et al.}~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime}. Our contribution is not a new detector and not the observation that transfer degrades. It is a single, controlled evaluation of one detector family in which these effects are measured together, with trivial baselines evaluated on every corpus, so that their relative sizes can be compared and the claims that commonly rest on them can be tested. Specifically:

\begin{itemize}
\item We measure a ladder of evaluation protocols on one pipeline, with six architectures and two input-blind baselines. A model-free nearest-neighbor probe and two sequence models attribute the random-split gain to the recognition of capture sessions (Section~\ref{sec:res:protocol}), and a split that fixes category coverage shows that the radio corpus supports no drift estimate, while the benign session in the test set sets the false positive rate (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}).
\item We audit the target corpus and show that half of 5G-NIDD's flows sit on records that carry both labels, and that a published 99.9\% result on it rests on record-position fields; we take that published pipeline down the same ladder (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}).
\item We separate prior shift, label conflicts and transfer failure in the cross-corpus gap with a prevalence-invariant metric, a difference-in-differences test against the majority baseline, references measured inside the target, and evaluation with and without the conflicting records, and we repeat the transfer on a third corpus from a different site and exporter (Sections~\ref{sec:res:transfer}, \ref{sec:res:anatomy} and~\ref{sec:res:third}).
\item We report operational precision and alert volume from pooled confusion counts in explicit units with cluster-bootstrap intervals, and show that averaging precision over evaluation folds inflates the apparent difference between architectures by an order of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}).
\item We show which calibration methods can change the achievable operating points, that none repairs them here, and that label-free estimation of the target prior fails when class-conditional distributions shift (Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration}).
\item We decompose decision latency on one host for three implementations, including a compiled runtime (Section~\ref{sec:res:latency}), and state a deployability test that separates a predictive evaluation from a non-predictive one, with reporting requirements (Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}

All latency figures are emulated: no RIC is in the path. The two corpora were produced by different flow exporters, so every cross-corpus gap spans a change of deployment and a change of tooling; Section~\ref{sec:res:anatomy} measures what that implies. The notation used throughout is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:symbols}.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section places the study in four bodies of work: the RIC as a platform for detection, O-RAN security, datasets and learning methods for network intrusion detection, and the literature on evaluation practice and dataset shift that the paper applies.

\subsection{The Near-Real-Time RIC as a Detection Platform}

xApps subscribe to E2 nodes through service models: E2SM-KPM delivers periodic key performance measurements and E2SM-RC exposes control actions~\cite{polese2023understanding}. Platforms such as OpenRAN Gym~\cite{bonati2023openrangym}, SCOPE~\cite{bonati2021scope}, ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran} and FlexRIC~\cite{schmidt2021flexric}, together with open radio stacks~\cite{nikaein2014oai,gomez2016srslte}, make experimental xApps practical. E2 carries aggregated measurements rather than packets, which matters for the data path of a flow-level detector (Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice}).

\subsection{Security of Open RAN}

The O-RAN Alliance's threat model~\cite{oran_wg11_security} and independent analyses~\cite{liyanage2023openran,mimran2022security} identify the RIC and third-party xApps as a new attack surface, and security mechanisms across O-RAN interfaces have been implemented and measured~\cite{groen2025implementing}. On the detection side, 5G-Spector runs layer-3 attack detection as an O-RAN compliant service~\cite{wen2024spector}, Det-RAN detects attacks from cross-layer data in real time~\cite{scalingi2024detran}, and Fard \emph{et al.} study the fusion of radio telemetry and flow records on the same source corpus we use~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}. These works propose or evaluate detectors on one deployment; our question is what evidence would show that such a detector works elsewhere.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Network Intrusion Detection}

Many IoT-oriented benchmark corpora exist~\cite{koroniotis2019botiot,moustafa2021toniot,ferrag2022edgeiiot,neto2023ciciot,sharafaldin2018cicids}. Cellular corpora are rarer: 5G-NIDD was captured on a functioning 5G test network~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, and NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD pairs flow records with radio telemetry from an O-RAN testbed~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}. On 5G-NIDD, the dataset's authors report binary accuracy of 99.85\% to 99.95\% for four of five classifiers on a random 70/30 split~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv}, and later work reports weighted $F_1$ of up to 99.95\%~\cite{ilias2024moe5g}. Standardized NetFlow feature sets make cross-corpus experiments possible~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}. The quality of benchmark corpora is itself a source of error: labeling and flow-construction faults have been documented in CIC-IDS2017~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}, and design flaws that let a classifier score well without learning the attack recur across NIDS benchmarks~\cite{flood2024smells}.

\subsection{Evaluation Practice and Dataset Shift}

Intrusion detection results are often over-optimistic because of closed-world evaluation~\cite{sommer2010outside}, base-rate neglect~\cite{axelsson2000baserate}, sampling bias and leakage~\cite{arp2022dosdonts,kapoor2023leakage}, and temporal or spatial bias in the split~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}; the gap between reported and operational effectiveness is surveyed in~\cite{apruzzese2023role}, and industrial intrusion detection shows the same pattern when detectors are re-evaluated on data they were not tuned on~\cite{kus2022falsesense}. Cross-dataset generalization of such detectors is poor~\cite{dhooge2020interdataset,layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. Closest to this paper, Abraheem and Edhirig transfer XGBoost between our two corpora on 15 harmonized features and report zero-shot balanced accuracy per attack family of 0.50 to 0.56 from NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD to 5G-NIDD and 0.52 to 0.83 in the reverse direction~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}. Our transfer results agree with theirs in direction and size. What this paper adds is the protocol ladder with input-blind baselines, references measured inside the target corpus, a published pipeline taken down the same ladder, an audit of the target's labels, and the operational and latency criteria. The dataset-shift literature supplies tools this paper uses: label-shift estimation from unlabeled data~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,lipton2018bbse}, calibration and its behavior under shift~\cite{guo2017calibration,ovadia2019trust,fawcett2007pav}, shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing}, and covariance alignment for domain adaptation~\cite{sun2016coral}. For comparing classifiers under repeated resampling we follow Nadeau and Bengio~\cite{nadeau2003inference}.

\section{Deployment Criteria and Threat Model}
\label{sec:criteria}

This section defines the system and threat model and the three quantities the evaluation measures, then combines them into a deployability test.

\subsection{System and Threat Model}

A near-real-time RIC serves a set of E2 nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), with user devices, IoT devices among them, attached through those nodes. The adversary controls a subset of devices and uses them for flooding, scanning, brute-force login or web attacks, the categories present in our corpora. The adversary does not control the RIC, the E2 interface or the xApp. The defender's xApp consumes either radio telemetry windows delivered over E2SM-KPM or flow records produced by a flow exporter co-located with the user plane, and may act through E2SM-RC. Because E2 does not carry packets, a flow-level detector needs such an exporter outside the RIC; its cost and placement are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice}.

\subsection{Criterion 1: Generalization Across Deployments}

Let \DA{} and \DB{} be independently collected corpora over a shared feature space. A model $f_{\theta}$ trained on \DA{} is characterized by the gap between its held-out score on \DA{} and its score on \DB{}. Because the two corpora have different attack prevalence (Table~\ref{tab:corpora}), a gap in macro-$F_1$ mixes a change in the classifier's behavior with a change in class balance: even a classifier that ignores its input changes macro-$F_1$ when prevalence changes. We therefore report the gap in balanced accuracy, which a change of prevalence cannot move,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{BA}} = \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\mathrm{test}}\right) - \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{B}\right),
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
together with the macro-$F_1$ gap $\Delta_{F_1}$ and its difference from the gap of the majority-class baseline. We quantify covariate shift feature by feature as
\begin{equation}
\bar{W}_{1} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} W_{1}\!\left(Q_{j}(P_{A}^{(j)}),\, Q_{j}(P_{B}^{(j)})\right),
\label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{j}$ is the quantile transform of feature $j$ fitted on source training data only. Normalizing each corpus by its own quantiles would map both marginals to the uniform distribution and make every term zero.

\subsection{Criterion 2: Alert Burden Under a Realistic Base Rate}

Let $\lambda_{b}$ be the benign arrival rate in the detector's own unit: flow records per hour for a flow-level detector, and telemetry windows per hour for a window-level detector. A benign UE produces 225 windows of 16\,s per hour. The false alerts per hour are
\begin{equation}
A(\tau) = \lambda_{b}\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau),
\label{eq:alerts}
\end{equation}
and, for an operational attack prevalence $\pi$, the fraction of alerts that are true is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PPV}(\tau) = \frac{\pi R(\tau)}{\pi R(\tau) + (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)}.
\label{eq:ppv}
\end{equation}
Neither $\pi$ nor $\lambda_{b}$ can be measured from the corpora; both are declared and swept. $\mathrm{PPV}$ depends on $\pi$ only, and $A$ scales linearly in $\lambda_{b}$, so we also report false alerts per $10^{6}$ benign flows, which does not depend on $\lambda_b$.

\subsection{Criterion 3: Latency Against the Control Budget}

The decision latency of an xApp decomposes as
\begin{equation}
L = t_{\mathrm{ind}} + t_{\mathrm{feat}} + t_{\mathrm{inf}} + t_{\mathrm{act}} + t_{q},
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
for indication decoding, feature construction, inference, control encoding and queueing. We measure $t_{\mathrm{feat}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{inf}}$ only; the other three terms need a RIC in the path. Conformance is a tail property: for budget $B$ and tolerated violation rate $\varepsilon$ we require $q_{1-\varepsilon}(L) \le B$. The O-RAN specification allows $B$ anywhere from 10\,ms to 1\,s~\cite{oran_wg3_ricarch}, so we report the smallest conforming budget rather than a verdict at one chosen value.

\subsection{A Deployability Test}

The three criteria combine into a test that a detector passes or fails:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{P}(f_{\theta}) = {} & \ind\!\left[\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}} \le \delta\right] \cdot \ind\!\left[\overline{q}_{0.99}(L) \le B\right] \\
& \cdot \ind\!\big[\exists\,\tau:\ R(\tau)\ge r_{\min},\ A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \\
& \qquad\quad \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho\big],
\end{split}
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}}$ and $\overline{q}_{0.99}$ are upper 95\% confidence bounds, so a pass cannot rest on a lucky point estimate, and every alert condition is evaluated on the target corpus. The minimum recall $r_{\min}$ prevents the alert term from being satisfied by a detector that never alerts. The operating threshold is the most sensitive admissible one,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\tau^{\star} = \arg\max_{\tau} R(\tau) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad & R(\tau) \ge r_{\min},\ A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \\
& \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho .
\end{split}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
The bounds are operator requirements, and Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate} states the values we use and how the verdict depends on them.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the corpora and feature spaces, the detectors, the evaluation protocols and the statistical analysis.

\subsection{Corpora}

\DA{} is NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}, captured on a physical OpenAirInterface O-RAN testbed with benign traffic and five attack categories: denial of service (DoS), distributed denial of service (DDoS), probing, brute force and web attacks. It provides two layers that cannot be joined record by record from the published files: flow records exported by Zeek from the central unit, and 1\,Hz radio telemetry from the distributed unit. We use both. The radio layer is aggregated into windows of 16 consecutive records of one UE, summarized by the mean and standard deviation of 16 KPM fields. Its 30 capture sessions are recovered from gaps of more than five minutes in the clock. Each recovered session carries a single category label in every record, so a radio label is a session label: every window of an attack session is an attack window. The sessions follow the capture schedule (Table~\ref{tab:benign}): nine benign sessions on days 0 to 6, the attack categories in blocks on days 47 to 53, and one benign session at the end. \DB{} is 5G-NIDD~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, captured on a 5G test network at a different institution, with flow records exported by Argus and two attack categories, DoS and probing. Its published files carry no addresses, ports or timestamps, but they keep Argus's record counter and file offset. The offset falls back to its starting value at every file boundary, which recovers 20 capture files: two passes over the same ten captures, one per base station (the published \texttt{Combined.csv} also restarts its row index where the second pass begins). Every attack file mixes benign traffic with one attack type. A third corpus, \DC{}, is a second transfer target from a third site: the flow records of a 5G core testbed (Open5GS in Docker) published by Nugraha \emph{et al.}~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}. Only three of its files are flow records, each with one attack (a SYN flood, an ICMP flood and a PFCP session-deletion attack), \ThNflows{} flows with \ThNattack{} attacks; its benign flows are user traffic and the core's own signalling. It was exported by NFStream, a third flow exporter, which counts link-layer bytes and, for flows inside the GTP-U user-plane tunnel, the 44 bytes of encapsulation; we remove both per packet so that the shared columns hold the inner packet's IP bytes, as for \DA{}. \subsection{Shared Feature Space}

Zeek and Argus share no column names, so the flow feature space is defined by meaning (Table~\ref{tab:features}): 15 concepts realized as 18 columns, namely seven counts each exporter reports, a one-hot protocol with a fixed level list, and seven derived quantities computed identically on both sides. One mapping deserves stating because the obvious choice is wrong: Zeek's \texttt{src\_bytes} counts application payload, which is zero for most floods and scans, whereas Argus's \texttt{SrcBytes} counts whole packets. The counterpart of \texttt{SrcBytes} is Zeek's \texttt{src\_ip\_bytes}, at 40 against 42 bytes per packet. A floor on duration and $\log(1+x)$ on unbounded quantities are applied identically to both corpora; scaling is fitted on source training data only. No address, port or timestamp is used as a feature.

We also define, before any result under it was computed, an exporter-robust subset of nine columns: the protocol and the five per-packet means and direction ratios. The two exporters disagree about where a flow ends, which moves duration, rates and totals. Per-packet means and direction ratios move less, but they are not invariant: cutting a flow changes them whenever its packets differ, for example when handshake and teardown packets fall into different pieces, and the two exporters count the same packet at 40 and 42 bytes, a fixed offset of about 5\% for minimum-size packets.

\subsection{Detectors}

We evaluate six supervised architectures and two trivial baselines on every protocol: LR, a depth-12 DT, an RF~\cite{breiman2001random}, XGB~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, scikit-learn's HGB~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit} and an MLP, against a majority-class predictor and a stratified sampler of the training prior. The baselines ignore their input, so any change in their score between two protocols is a change in class composition alone. Every supervised model receives the same class-imbalance policy: balanced class weights, applied as sample weights for the MLP and as \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} for XGB. Hyperparameters are fixed, not tuned (Table~\ref{tab:hyper}).

Three further families answer specific questions. Two sequence models, a GRU~\cite{cho2014gru} and a one-dimensional convolutional network, read the raw 16-step KPM sequences of the same radio windows. Two unsupervised detectors, an isolation forest~\cite{liu2008isolation} and an autoencoder, are trained on benign source flows only, with the alarm threshold fixed at the 99th percentile of source-benign scores. A CORAL arm~\cite{sun2016coral} re-colors source flows to the covariance of the unlabeled target before training; it is the only experiment in which a detector sees target data.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}

Splits are group-disjoint wherever a group key exists, and a guard raises if a group appears on both sides. On the radio layer the key is the capture session, and we compare three protocols: a random split of windows; a run-disjoint split; and a category-stratified run-disjoint split that keeps sessions of every attack category on both sides, so that no test category is unseen in training. Two further designs address time (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}): the latest or the earliest session of every category held out, against draws that hold out a random session of every category, and each benign session held out alone. On the flow layer the key is the source address (318 groups); each of 20 split seeds draws a 300,000-flow subsample of \DA{} and a source-address-disjoint split of it. Every model is then evaluated on the whole of \DB{}, and the reverse direction trains on a random split of \DB{} and evaluates on \DA{}. On \DB{} itself, in-target references hold out four of the 20 capture files, or train on one base station's pass and test on the other's (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}). Except for these references, CORAL, the domain classifier and the prior estimates of Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration}, all of which are labeled as such, no transform, threshold or model choice uses \DB{}, and every read of it is logged.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Uncertainty is estimated over 20 split seeds. The splits resample one population of 30 sessions or 318 source addresses, so their training and test sets overlap and a paired $t$-test over split means understates the variance. We therefore report Nadeau--Bengio corrected intervals~\cite{nadeau2003inference}, which scale the variance by $1/J + n_{\mathrm{test}}/n_{\mathrm{train}}$ for $J$ splits. Holm correction is applied within each family of tests, where a family is one metric and one comparison (a protocol pair or a transfer direction) across the six supervised architectures; the two sequence models form a family of their own. Operating points are computed from confusion counts pooled over folds; Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts} shows why averaging per-fold precision is wrong. Pooled counts repeat a sample in every seed that tests it, and samples of one cluster are correlated, so a binomial interval on them is far too narrow. Intervals for pooled operating points therefore come from a cluster bootstrap with 2,000 replicates that resamples, with replacement, both the split seeds and the clusters: radio sessions, \DA{} source addresses or \DB{} capture files.

A decision that misses the budget is still acted on and recorded as a violation, since a late mitigation is usually better than none; an operator who prefers to drop late actions moves the send inside the deadline check.

\Ensure E2 control actions; violation count $v$; latency record

  \EndIf

  \If{$\ell > B - \delta_{m}$}

  \State $\textsc{RecordLatency}(\ell)$ \Comment{every indication}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All measurements were taken on one workstation: a 13th Gen Intel Core i9-13900H (14 cores, 20 threads), 47.6\,GB of memory, Windows 11 Pro build 26120, Python 3.11.9, scikit-learn 1.8.0, XGBoost 3.2.0, NumPy 2.3.5 and ONNX Runtime 1.30.0; the sequence models used PyTorch 2.14 on the CPU in a separate environment. A complete package list is committed with the results. No RIC was involved: we did not deploy the detector as an xApp, run an E2 agent or containerize anything, and all latency figures are emulated.

The radio-layer protocol comparison uses 20 split seeds with two model seeds each; the other radio-layer and flow-layer experiments use 20 split seeds with one model seed; calibration uses 10 split seeds of a three-way source split into training, calibration and test groups. The declared operational parameters are an attack prevalence $\pi = 0.002$ and a benign arrival rate $\lambda_{b} = 240{,}000$ flows per hour, both swept; for the radio layer we report false alerts per benign UE-hour. Latency is timed per call with a monotonic clock, with garbage collection enabled, after 1,000 warm-up calls, over 20,000 calls, on one thread per library; distribution-free confidence intervals are given for every quantile.

\subsection{Evaluation-Protocol Sensitivity}
\label{sec:res:protocol}

Table~\ref{tab:leakage} and Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage} compare three protocols on the radio layer. A random split raises macro-$F_1$ over a run-disjoint split by \LeakMin{} to \LeakMax{} across the six architectures, \LeakAvg{} on average, and the gain is positive in at least \LeakSeedsMin{} of 20 splits for every architecture. The trivial baselines gain nothing. The corrected interval for the average gain is [\LeakAvgNbLo, \LeakAvgNbHi] ($p = \LeakAvgPnb$): with 30 capture sessions the size of the effect cannot be pinned down, and no single architecture's gain survives Holm correction. A naive paired $t$-test would report $p = \LeakAvgPt$ for the same data.

The gain does not come from test categories absent in training: in all 20 run-disjoint splits every test category also appeared in training. The category-stratified protocol guarantees this by construction and gives a gain of \LeakStratMin{} to \LeakStratMax{}; it moves sessions of rare categories into the test set, which makes its test sets harder, so we do not read its larger gain as further evidence. A model-free probe points to the mechanism. Under a random split, \NNSame\% of test windows have their nearest training window in the same capture session (\NNSameMin\% to \NNSameMax\% across splits), and a one-nearest-neighbor (1-NN) lookup scores \NNoneRandom{} macro-$F_1$ against \NNoneRun{} under a run-disjoint split (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). Because every radio label is a session label, recognizing the session is enough to predict the label, and a random split lets any model, including a lookup table, do so. Under the run-disjoint split the lookup scores above LR and DT and within \NNgapBest{} of the best supervised architecture, so on this layer the learned models add little to the retrieval of similar windows. The two sequence models of Table~\ref{tab:sequence}, which read the raw 16-step KPM sequences instead of window summaries, show the same pattern more clearly: a random split scores \SeqRandomMin{} to \SeqRandomMax{} against \SeqRDmin{} to \SeqRDmax{} under a run-disjoint split, a gain of \SeqGainMin{} to \SeqGainMax{} whose corrected intervals exclude zero for both models (Holm-adjusted $p \le \SeqPholmMax{}$ within the pair).

\subsection{Time Order and Benign Sessions}
\label{sec:res:drift}

The radio layer spans \DriftDays{} days, but its capture order follows the scenario schedule (Table~\ref{tab:benign}). Nine benign sessions were recorded on days 0 to 6, the five attack categories in blocks on days 47 to 53, and one last benign session on day 53. Every radio label is a session label: a session is benign, or one attack category, throughout. A split on session order therefore holds out whole attack categories. At training fractions of 0.4 to 0.7, the latest sessions contain \DvAuditUnseenMin{} to \DvAuditUnseenMax{} attack categories absent from training, and a single benign session. Such a split measures the detection of unseen categories, so we do not report it as drift.

To hold category coverage fixed, we held out the latest session of every category, benign included, and compared the result with \DvNctrl{} draws that hold out a random session of every category (Table~\ref{tab:timesplit}). With the latest sessions held out, balanced accuracy is \DvFwdBAmin{} to \DvFwdBAmax{}, against control means of \DvCtrlBAmin{} to \DvCtrlBAmax{}, yet \DvFwdPctMin\% to \DvFwdPctMax\% of control draws score as low. With the earliest sessions held out, it is \DvRevBAmin{} to \DvRevBAmax{}, which \DvRevPctMin\% to \DvRevPctMax\% of control draws match or exceed. Time order does not move the result outside ordinary session-to-session variation.

The benign session in the test set decides it. Held out one at a time (Table~\ref{tab:benign}), \BenLowN{} of the ten benign sessions draw a false positive rate of at most \BenLowMax{}, while sessions \BenHighSessions{} (days \BenHighDays{}) draw \BenHighMin{} to \BenHighMax{}. The last benign session, the only one recorded during the attack period, draws \BenLateFPR{}, or \BenLateFA{} false alerts per benign UE-hour, but two sessions from the first week draw as much. With ten benign sessions, a change over time cannot be separated from this heterogeneity. The practical consequence is that a detector's false positive rate on a new site cannot be read off its rate on the benign sessions it was tested on.

\subsection{The Target Corpus: Labels and a Published Result}
\label{sec:res:target}

\emph{Label conflicts.} Before transferring to \DB{}, we asked whether its labels are a function of its flow records. For half of the corpus they are not. Of the \NDbFlows{} flows, \LcShareAll\% (\LcFlowsAll) share every field except the row index, \texttt{Seq} and \texttt{Offset} with a flow of the opposite label (Table~\ref{tab:conflict}). All of them lie in the two UDP-flood captures, \LcBenignFileConf\% of file~\LcBenignFile{} and \LcAttackFileConf\% of file~\LcAttackFile{}, one from each base station. The pattern is specific: each of the \LcTwinAttack{} flows labeled UDP flood in file~\LcAttackFile{} has an identical copy labeled benign in file~\LcBenignFile{}, in equal numbers for \LcTwinEqual{} of the \LcTwinRecords{} distinct records. The same traffic appears to have been captured at both stations and labeled differently at each; the copies make up \LcBenignTwinPct\% of the corpus's benign class. We cannot tell from the published files which label is correct, so every \DB{} result below is given with and without these benign copies, and no label is changed.

The conflicts set a ceiling on every classifier that reads the record. The best any function of the \LcNFeat{} content fields can do, even evaluated on the flows it was built from, is a balanced accuracy of \LcCeilNative{}; in the 18 shared columns the ceiling is \LcCeilShared{}, which is where DT, XGB, HGB and the MLP sit when trained and tested on a random split of \DB{} (Table~\ref{tab:transfer_rev}). With \texttt{Seq} and \texttt{Offset} added, every record becomes unique and the ceiling is \LcCeilPos{}.

\emph{References inside the target.} To separate what transfer costs from what \DB{} itself allows, we trained and tested the six architectures on \DB{} alone (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}). On a random split they reach \TgtRandShBAmin{} to \TgtRandShBAmax{} in the shared space, the ceiling above, and \TgtRandShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtRandShCleanBAmax{} when the benign copies are left out of the test set. On held-out capture files they reach \TgtFileShBAmin{} to \TgtFileShBAmax{} in the shared space and \TgtFileNatBAmin{} to \TgtFileNatBAmax{} with native features; none of the three draws held out a UDP-flood capture. Across the two base stations, within one corpus and one exporter, balanced accuracy falls to \TgtSiteABShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteABShBAmax{} from station 1 to station 2 and \TgtSiteBAShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShBAmax{} in the other direction. Both failures come from the copies: trained on station 1, whose labels call the flood copies benign, a model misses station 2's floods; trained on station 2, it flags station 1's benign-labeled copies, and without them in the test set it scores \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmax. On flows whose label the record determines, then, the shared space separates \DB{} almost perfectly, and the target is not what limits transfer.

\subsection{Transfer Across Corpora}
\label{sec:res:transfer}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer} report transfer from \DA{} to \DB{}. In macro-$F_1$ every supervised model loses \TrFoneDropMin{} to \TrFoneDropMax{}, but the majority baseline, which reads no features, loses \TrMajDrop{} as well, because \DB{} has a lower attack prevalence than \DA{}. Measured against that baseline the macro-$F_1$ loss is between \TrDiDmin{} and \TrDiDmax{}, and after correction none of the macro-$F_1$ losses is significant. Macro-$F_1$ is the wrong instrument here. Balanced accuracy, which prevalence cannot move, shows the loss clearly: from \TrSrcBAmin{}--\TrSrcBAmax{} on held-out source data to \TrTgtBAmin{}--\TrTgtBAmax{} on \DB{}, a drop of \TrBAdropMin{} to \TrBAdropMax{} that is significant for \TrSigBA{} of six architectures after correction, is positive in at least \TrBAseedsMin{} of 20 splits for every architecture, and averages \TrBAdropAvg{} [\TrBAdropAvgNbLo, \TrBAdropAvgNbHi]. The floors do not move. Target ROC-AUC lies between \TrTgtAUCmin{} and \TrTgtAUCmax{}.

The detectors remain above chance on the target, but not far above it: target balanced accuracy averages \TrTgtBAavg{}, and for \TrChanceSig{} of six architectures its corrected interval excludes 0.5. For reference, the stratified sampler scores \TrStratFloor{} macro-$F_1$ on \DB{} and a fair coin would score \TrFairCoin{}; we report both because the value of a macro-$F_1$ floor depends on which prior it samples. LR transfers best by every prevalence-free measure (balanced accuracy \TrLRtgtBA{}, ROC-AUC \TrLRtgtAUC{}); the MLP, best on held-out source balanced accuracy (\TrMLPsrcBA{}), reaches \TrMLPtgtBA{} on the target. Source performance does not predict target performance in a prevalence-free metric: across the six architectures the rank correlation is \RhoBA{} in balanced accuracy ($p=\RhoBAP$). In macro-$F_1$ it is \RhoFone{} ($p=\RhoFoneP$), but macro-$F_1$ ranks move with prevalence, and six architectures give neither value much precision.

\emph{The target's labels.} Part of this loss is the label conflict of Section~\ref{sec:res:target}. The conflicts therefore reverse which detector looks transferable: on all flows LR looks best, on flows whose label the record determines it looks worst. Against the target's own reference on those flows (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}), transfer still falls short by \TrCleanShortMin{} to \TrCleanShortMax{} of balanced accuracy.

\subsection{What the Transfer Gap Is Made Of}
\label{sec:res:anatomy}

\emph{Separability.} A classifier trained to tell a \DA{} flow from a \DB{} flow reaches \DomShared{} balanced accuracy on the 18 shared columns and \DomRobust{} on the nine exporter-robust columns alone. The largest per-feature shifts are the protocol mix (most \DA{} flows are TCP and most \DB{} flows UDP), the direction ratios and the packet counts, and the robust columns shift as much as the segmentation-dependent ones ($\bar{W}_1$ of \WoneRobust{} against \WoneNonrobust). The corpora therefore differ in traffic composition, which no choice of exporter would remove, as well as in flow segmentation, which a common exporter would.

\emph{Model families and adaptation.} Table~\ref{tab:arms} evaluates the same splits under three changes, and none produces a transferable detector. Restricting the detectors to the nine exporter-robust columns raises held-out source balanced accuracy to \RobSrcBAmin{} to \RobSrcBAmax{}, yet target balanced accuracy falls to \RobBAmin{} to \RobBAmax{}, no better than chance: the columns that do not depend on flow segmentation carry the traffic-composition shift measured above. CORAL, which reads unlabeled target features, gives target balanced accuracy of \CoralBAmin{} to \CoralBAmax{}; only the MLP gains (\CoralMLPtgtBA{} against \TrMLPtgtBA{} without adaptation), and the target false positive rate rises to \CoralFPRmin{} to \CoralFPRmax{}. The benign-only novelty detectors fail already on held-out source data: in this flow space, floods and scans resemble short benign flows.

\emph{Cost of the shared space.} Projecting \DA{} onto the 18 shared columns costs between \HarmBAmin{} and \HarmBAmax{} of in-distribution balanced accuracy relative to all \NFullFeat{} non-port Zeek features (Table~\ref{tab:harm}). The low source macro-$F_1$ of Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reflects the \PrevDa\% attack prevalence of \DA{} rather than weak detectors; their source balanced accuracy is \TrSrcBAmin{} to \TrSrcBAmax.

\subsection{A Third Corpus}
\label{sec:res:third}

To see whether these results depend on the particular pair of corpora, we added \DC{}, the flow records of a 5G core testbed~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}, as a second target. It changes the site, the flow exporter and the vantage point at once, and unlike \DB{} its labels are consistent: only \ThConfShare\% of its flows sit on records that also carry the opposite label, and the balanced-accuracy ceiling in the shared columns is \ThCeilShared{}. Trained and tested on \DC{} itself, the six architectures reach \ThRefShRandBAmin{} to \ThRefShRandBAmax{} on a random split, and \ThRefShLofoBAmin{} to \ThRefShLofoBAmax{} when the capture file of one attack type is held out, so that the attack is unseen.

Transferred from \DA{} (Table~\ref{tab:third}, 20 split seeds), balanced accuracy on \DC{} is \ThATgtBAmin{} to \ThATgtBAmax{} and ROC-AUC \ThATgtAUCmin{} to \ThATgtAUCmax. Every architecture detects the SYN flood, a kind of attack it was trained on; recall on the ICMP flood ranges from \ThAIcmpMin{} to \ThAIcmpMax{}, and on the control-plane PFCP attack from \ThAPfcpMin{} to \ThAPfcpMax. What fails is the new site's normal traffic: the false positive rate is \ThAFPRmin{} to \ThAFPRmax{}, and up to \ThABgFlagMax{} of the core's own signalling is flagged. The loss from held-out source data, \ThADropMin{} to \ThADropMax{}, varies so much between split seeds that none is significant after correction. Transferred from \DB{} (10 split seeds), balanced accuracy spreads from \ThBTgtBAmin{} to \ThBTgtBAmax{}, with ROC-AUC as low as \ThBTgtAUCmin{}: which architecture transfers depends on the pair of corpora, and none does so consistently. At $\pi = 0.002$ the best operational precision at any threshold keeping recall above 10\% is \ThAPPVbest{} from \DA{} and \ThBPPVbest{} from \DB{}, and no architecture passes the deployability test in either direction (\ThAPredPass{} and \ThBPredPass{} of six). On a third site, with a third exporter and consistent labels, the pattern of Section~\ref{sec:res:transfer} recurs: the SYN flood is found, and the site's normal traffic is not recognized as normal.

\subsection{Alert Burden}
\label{sec:res:alerts}

Table~\ref{tab:pooled} gives pooled operating points at $\tau=0.5$ on three evaluation sets, with 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals. On the radio layer, the six detectors have a pooled window false positive rate of \RadFPRmin{} to \RadFPRmax{}, or \RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} false alerts per benign UE-hour, and at $\pi=0.002$ an operational precision of \RadPPVmin{} to \RadPPVmax{} against a corpus precision of \RadPrecMin{} to \RadPrecMax{}. These point estimates are not stable. The false positive rate is set by which of the ten benign sessions land in the test set (Table~\ref{tab:benign}), and the bootstrap interval of the pooled rate runs from \RadCbFPRlo{} to \RadCbFPRhi{} across architectures, or \RadCbFAlo{} to \RadCbFAhi{} false alerts per benign UE-hour; operational precision at $\tau=0.5$ runs from \RadCbPPVlo{} to \RadCbPPVhi{}. Thirty sessions do not determine the radio layer's alert burden; they show only that it depends on the benign conditions a detector meets. On held-out \DA{} flows the false positive rate is \NetSrcFPRmin{} to \NetSrcFPRmax{}, which at $\lambda_b = 240{,}000$ benign flows per hour is \NetSrcFAmin{} to \NetSrcFAmax{} false alerts per hour; on \DB{} it is \NetTgtFPRmin{} to \NetTgtFPRmax{}, or \NetTgtFAmin{} to \NetTgtFAmax{} per hour. Without the benign copies of Section~\ref{sec:res:target} it is \NetCleanFPRmin{} to \NetCleanFPRmax{}, still \NetCleanFAmin{} to \NetCleanFAmax{} false alerts per hour, and operational precision is \NetCleanPPVmin{} to \NetCleanPPVmax{}.

Raising the threshold helps only on held-out \DA{} flows (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}). The best threshold is chosen on the evaluation data, so the values below are upper bounds. With recall held above 10\%, high thresholds reach an operational precision of \NetSrcPPVbest{} there, but the best any detector reaches on \DB{} is \NetTgtPPVbest{}, and \NetCleanPPVbest{} without the copies. On the radio layer the best pooled value is \RadPPVbest{}, but its bootstrap interval reaches 1.0 for \RadCbBestHiN{} of six architectures: a resample that omits the benign sessions flagged almost entirely leaves thresholds without a single false positive. The step from corpus to operational precision is arithmetic in $\pi$: sweeping $\pi$ from $10^{-4}$ to 0.5 moves the collapse factor from \RadCollapseLowPi{} to \RadCollapseHighPi{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:prevalence}), so an operational precision means nothing without the prevalence it assumes.
\emph{How operational precision should be estimated.} PPV is strongly non-linear in the false positive rate near zero, so a fold that happens to contain no false positive yields $\mathrm{PPV}=1$ whatever its recall. Averaging per-fold PPV therefore rewards the architecture that draws the most such folds. On the radio layer the same counts give a spread between the best and worst architecture of \EstPooled{} when pooled, \EstMedian{} as a median over folds and \EstMean{} as a mean over folds (Table~\ref{tab:estimator}). We report pooled counts throughout.

\subsection{Calibration and Prior Shift}
\label{sec:res:calibration}

Under transfer the scores are poorly calibrated (Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}). We fitted three calibrators on a group-disjoint source calibration fold (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). Whether a calibrator can change the achievable operating points depends on whether it preserves the order of scores. Temperature scaling does, and preserves ROC-AUC to within \CalTempMax{}. Platt scaling is monotone but its fitted slope can be negative; for DT in \CalPlattFlipSeeds{} of \CalSeeds{} seeds it inverted the ranking and changed ROC-AUC by \CalPlattFlip. In that seed the tree's scores carried no ranking information on the calibration fold (ROC-AUC \PlattCalAUC{} on \PlattCalGroups{} held-out source addresses) while they ranked the test fold well (\PlattTestAUC), so Platt scaling fitted the fold faithfully with a slope of \PlattSlope{} and inverted the test ranking. The failure belongs to small group-disjoint calibration folds, which need not resemble the test fold, rather than to Platt scaling. Isotonic regression is monotone but piecewise constant: it merges scores into ties and, on its fitting data, yields the ROC convex hull~\cite{fawcett2007pav}; its largest change in ROC-AUC ranged from \CalIsoMin{} to \CalIsoMax{} across architectures. None of the calibrators raises target macro-$F_1$ above the raw scores for any architecture: fitted on a source with \PrevDa\% attacks, they move scores toward that prior and flag more benign traffic on \DB{}.

The obvious remedy for prior shift is to rescale scores to the target prior~\cite{saerens2002adjusting}, which a deployment does not know. Two estimators recover it from unlabeled data under the assumption that only the class balance changes: expectation maximization~\cite{saerens2002adjusting} and black-box shift estimation~\cite{lipton2018bbse}. Here that assumption fails. Against a true target prior of \PriorTrue{}, their per-architecture mean estimates range from \PriorMeanMin{} to \PriorMeanMax{}, and \PriorFracExtreme\% of individual estimates fall below 0.05 or above 0.95. Rescaling with the true prior, an oracle, does not beat the raw scores either (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). The limit is discriminability on the target, with ROC-AUC of \TrTgtAUCmin{} to \TrTgtAUCmax{}, not calibration.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section draws the consequences for O-RAN practice, applies the deployability test, and relates the results to the reported state of the art.

\subsection{Implications for O-RAN Practice}
\label{sec:disc:practice}

\emph{Data path.} E2SM-KPM delivers aggregated measurements per reporting period; it does not carry packets. A window-level detector can therefore run inside the RIC on KPM reports, but a flow-level detector needs a flow exporter next to the user plane, in the central unit or at the user-plane function, and a transport of its records to the xApp, for example through a custom E2 service model or the non-real-time data collection path. That transport adds to $t_{\mathrm{ind}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:latency}, which we did not measure.

\emph{Detection delay.} Compute latency is a small part of the time to detect. A window-level decision cannot precede the end of its window (16\,s here), and a flow-level decision cannot precede the export of the flow record, which for a long connection waits for an idle or active timeout. The KPM reporting period and the flow timeouts should be reported beside compute latency.

\emph{Acting on alerts.} An alert that drives an E2SM-RC action, such as isolating a UE or steering it, acts on a false alert as readily as on a true one. At the false positive rates of Table~\ref{tab:pooled}, automatic mitigation on the radio layer would act against each benign UE \RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} times per hour; alerts of this quality can inform an operator or a non-real-time policy through A1, but should not trigger automatic control.

\emph{Admission and monitoring.} The deployability test of Eq.~\eqref{eq:deploy} can serve as an admission check before an xApp is enabled on a new site, provided that site supplies labeled benign and attack traffic. After admission, unlabeled shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing} can flag when the incoming traffic has moved away from the training distribution; the corpora here are separable at \DomShared{} balanced accuracy, so such a monitor would fire immediately.

\subsection{Relation to Reported Results}

Published O-RAN and 5G intrusion detection results are mostly in-distribution scores on corpora we do not share, and our own in-distribution scores under a random split are high as well (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). A direct numerical comparison would therefore compare protocols rather than detectors. What the results above allow is a statement about which reported quantities predict deployment: held-out macro-$F_1$ under a random split does not, and the quantities in the reporting requirements of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} are those that changed the conclusions here.

\section{Reproducibility and Ethics}
\label{sec:repro}

Code, configuration, the seeded split procedures from which every split is regenerated deterministically, raw per-seed results, and the generators for every table, figure and in-text number are available at \url{https://github.com/adeliusa486/oran-ids-deployability}. One script regenerates every number, table and figure of this paper from the shipped per-seed results and checks them against the manuscript, and the same script re-runs each experiment from the raw corpora, whose files it verifies by SHA-256; the repository's \texttt{REPRODUCE.md} gives the steps. Both corpora are public: \DA{} under CC-BY-4.0 from its authors~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab} and \DB{} from its authors~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd}. The traffic comes from testbed devices and a test network, not from subscribers, so no personal data was processed. Publishing where detectors fail carries a modest dual-use risk; we judge it outweighed by the benefit to operators of knowing that reported accuracy does not transfer.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Exporter.} \DA{} and \DB{} were exported by different tools, and we could not re-extract either corpus with a common exporter: our copy of \DB{} contains Argus flow records only, and no Zeek installation was available on the measurement host. The domain classifier and the robust subset show that the corpora differ in traffic composition as well as in flow segmentation, but they do not measure how much of the transfer gap a common exporter would remove. The base-station split inside \DB{} (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}) changes the site while keeping the exporter, which bounds part of the question from one side.

\emph{Target labels.} Of \DB{}'s benign flows, \LcBenignTwinPct\% are exact copies of records labeled as UDP flood elsewhere in the corpus (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}). We cannot tell from the published files which label is correct, so every \DB{} operating point is reported with and without these flows. The assignment of the two capture passes to the two base stations is inferred from the repeated capture schedule and the restarted row index; the dataset does not state it.

\emph{Radio capture schedule.} Nine of the ten benign radio sessions were recorded six weeks before any attack session, mostly with one UE, and every radio label is a session label. A radio detector may therefore separate capture periods as well as attacks. The one benign session from the attack period is flagged almost entirely, but so are two early sessions (Table~\ref{tab:benign}), and ten benign sessions cannot separate the two explanations.

\emph{Three corpora.} Three corpora establish that the gap recurs, not how it varies across deployments. The third is a 5G core testbed rather than an O-RAN deployment, holds only floods and a control-plane attack, and has three capture files, too few for cluster intervals. No third O-RAN corpus with paired radio telemetry was available to us.

\emph{Latency.} All latency figures are emulated on one Windows host without CPU isolation, and $t_{\mathrm{ind}}$, $t_{\mathrm{act}}$ and $t_{q}$ are not measured. CPU and memory use under load are not measured.

\emph{Sample size.} The radio layer has 30 capture sessions, each with a single scenario, so session and attack scenario are confounded, and the protocol and temporal analyses can detect large effects only. With ten benign sessions, the radio layer's false positive rate, and with it its operational precision, carries a wide interval (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}). The transfer statistics vary training sets over 20 seeds but evaluate one fixed target corpus, so they do not describe other deployments.

\emph{Scope.} We evaluate binary detection. Multi-class attribution, feature attribution~\cite{lundberg2017shap} and the fusion of radio and flow features are left open; the published summary files cannot be joined record by record, and fusion requires the raw archives, as in~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}; three attack categories of \DA{} are absent from \DB{} and can be tested in one direction only.

\section{Conclusion and Reporting Requirements}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A held-out score on one corpus says little about a detection xApp's behavior elsewhere. On the corpora studied here, a random split inflates the score because models recognize capture sessions; the radio corpus's capture schedule leaves no room to estimate drift, and the benign session a detector meets sets its false positive rate; a published 99.9\% on the target corpus rests on record-position fields, and half of that corpus carries labels its records cannot determine. On flows with consistent labels, detectors transferred from the other corpus reach balanced accuracy \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}, below what the target allows; on a third corpus they detect the SYN flood and flag much of the normal traffic; and at a realistic base rate no threshold yields usable operational precision. We therefore propose that papers claiming a deployable O-RAN detection capability report at least: (i) performance on a corpus collected independently of the training data, in a prevalence-invariant metric such as balanced accuracy or ROC-AUC, beside input-blind baselines and a reference trained inside that corpus; (ii) the split protocol and its group key, results under a group-disjoint split, and the category coverage of any time-based split; (iii) the false positive rate on benign traffic, with its unit, the resulting alert rate, and the operational precision at a stated base rate, all from pooled confusion counts with intervals that resample clusters; (iv) the operating threshold, how it was chosen, and the recall it gives up; (v) intervals that account for overlap between resampled splits; (vi) tail latency with its stages, implementation and host, measured on a real RIC before any conformance claim is made; and (vii) evidence that the labels are a function of the features used, with no identical records under opposite labels, and that no record-position or identifier field is a feature.\bibliographystyle{IEEEtran}
