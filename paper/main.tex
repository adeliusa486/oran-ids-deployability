\begin{abstract}
Papers on intrusion detection for 5G and O-RAN often report more than 99\% accuracy on a random split of one corpus. We asked what such a number says about deployment. Our study uses two public corpora (NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD and 5G-NIDD), six architectures, two baselines that ignore their input, and increasingly strict test protocols. On the radio layer, a random split adds \LeakAvg{} to macro-$F_1$ (95\% interval \LeakAvgNbLo{} to \LeakAvgNbHi{}, significant only for two sequence models), and a nearest-neighbor lookup gains \NNGain{}, consistent with the recognition of capture sessions. False positive rates on held-out benign sessions range from 0.00 to \BenHighMax{}. In 5G-NIDD, \LcBenignTwinPct\% of the benign flows are copies of flood records labeled as attacks, and their addresses mark them as the flood of a second attacker. These copies cap balanced accuracy at \LcCeilNative{}. The published 99.9\% accuracy rests on two fields that encode a record's position in its file, and without them it drops to \PubOneNoposAccMax\%. On flows with consistent labels, transferred detectors reach a balanced accuracy of only \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}. Re-extracting the target with the source's exporter does not close this gap, and a third corpus behaves the same way. At an attack prevalence of 0.002, precision never exceeds \NetCleanPPVbest{} while recall stays above 10\%, and no detector passes our deployability test. Latency is the exception. In a FlexRIC near-real-time RIC with an emulated E2 node, a decision and its control message take at most \RicLoopTenPnnHiMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile. We close with reporting requirements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) splits the base station into radio, distributed, and central units joined by open interfaces, and it adds a RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) that runs third-party control applications~\cite{polese2023understanding,abdalla2022toward,niknam2022intelligent}. The near-real-time RIC sits close to the cells it controls~\cite{bonati2021intelligence,polese2023understanding}, so it sees the telemetry of every device those cells serve. That makes it an obvious place to look for attacks. Compromised devices, many of them IoT devices, are a well-documented source of volumetric attacks~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai,kolias2017ddos}, and a detection application (xApp) inside the RIC control loop could catch them close to where they enter the network. Platforms for building xApps exist~\cite{polese2023coloran,schmidt2021flexric}, and detectors that run in or beside the O-RAN control loop have already been shown to work~\cite{wen2024spector,scalingi2024detran}.

Most of these detectors are tested on a random held-out split of a single dataset, where accuracy or $F_1$ above 99\% is common. For 5G-NIDD, one of our two corpora, the dataset authors report binary accuracies of 99.85\% to 99.95\% for four of five classifiers~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv}, and later work reports a weighted $F_1$ of up to 99.95\%~\cite{ilias2024moe5g}. Such a split says little about deployment. Three properties decide whether a detector can be deployed, and the split measures none of them. The first is generalization to a site that contributed no training data, and intrusion detectors are known to transfer poorly between independently collected datasets~\cite{layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. The second is alert burden: a false positive rate that looks small on a balanced benchmark becomes a stream of alerts once it meets production flow rates and a realistic attack base rate~\cite{axelsson2000baserate,sommer2010outside}. The third is latency, because the O-RAN E2 specification requires support for control loops between 10\,ms and 1\,s~\cite{etsi_e2gap}. A benchmark score measures how well a detector separates the records of one corpus. Deployment asks a different question, and we treat a benchmark result as evidence about deployment only when an experiment supports that reading.

None of these concerns is new. Axelsson made the base-rate argument~\cite{axelsson2000baserate} and Sommer and Paxson the closed-world critique~\cite{sommer2010outside}. Arp et al. catalogued the common pitfalls of machine learning in security~\cite{arp2022dosdonts}, and Pendlebury et al. formalized temporal and spatial bias~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}. For the two corpora used here, Abraheem and Edhirig measured transfer~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}, and Obiuwevwi et al. timed inference inside a real near-real-time RIC~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime}. We offer neither a new detector nor the finding that transfer hurts. What we add is a single controlled evaluation of one detector family in which these effects are measured together, with input-blind baselines on every corpus, so that their sizes can be compared and the claims that rest on them can be checked. Our contributions are these:

\begin{itemize}
\item A ladder of evaluation protocols applied to one pipeline, six architectures, and two input-blind baselines. A model-free nearest-neighbor probe and two sequence models trace the random-split gain to the recognition of capture sessions (Section~\ref{sec:res:protocol}). Once category coverage is held fixed, the radio corpus supports no estimate of temporal drift, and its false positive rate depends mainly on which benign session is tested (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}).
\item An audit of the target corpus. Half of the 5G-NIDD flows lie on records that occur under both labels, the addresses of the benign copies identify them as the flood of a second attacker, and a published 99.9\% result depends on record-position fields. We then move the published pipeline down the same ladder (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}).
\item A breakdown of the cross-corpus gap into prior shift, label conflicts, and genuine transfer failure. It uses a prevalence-invariant metric, a difference-in-differences test against the majority baseline, references trained inside the target, and evaluation with and without the conflicting records. We also re-extract the target from its raw captures with the source's exporter, and we add a third corpus from another site with another exporter (Sections~\ref{sec:res:transfer}, \ref{sec:res:anatomy}, and~\ref{sec:res:third}).
\item Operational precision and alert volume from pooled confusion counts, in explicit units and with cluster-bootstrap intervals. Averaging precision over folds instead inflates the apparent difference between architectures by an order of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}).
\item An account of which calibration methods can change the reachable operating points. None of them helps here, and label-free estimates of the target prior fail once the class-conditional distributions shift (Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration}).
\item Decision latency on one host for three implementations, one of them a compiled runtime, and the full decision loop of the detector running as an xApp in a FlexRIC near-real-time RIC with an emulated E2 node (Section~\ref{sec:res:latency}). Finally, a deployability test that combines generalization, alert burden, and latency, an analysis of how its verdict depends on its bounds, and a set of reporting requirements (Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}

Two limits of scope apply throughout. We measured latency on one host, once without a RIC and once through FlexRIC with an emulated E2 node, so no radio or operator network took part. The three corpora also come from three different flow exporters, which means that every cross-corpus gap mixes a change of site with a change of tooling. For \DA{} and \DB{} we remove this confound by re-extracting \DB{} with the exporter of \DA{} (Section~\ref{sec:res:anatomy}). Table~\ref{tab:symbols} lists the notation.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Four strands of work matter here: the RIC as a place to run detectors, the security of O-RAN, the datasets and models of network intrusion detection, and the literature on evaluation practice and dataset shift that our methods borrow from.

\subsection{The Near-Real-Time RIC as a Detection Platform}

An xApp talks to E2 nodes through service models. E2SM-KPM delivers periodic key performance measurements, and E2SM-RC exposes control actions~\cite{polese2023understanding}. Building experimental xApps became practical with platforms such as OpenRAN Gym~\cite{bonati2023openrangym}, SCOPE~\cite{bonati2021scope}, ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran}, and FlexRIC~\cite{schmidt2021flexric}, together with open radio stacks~\cite{nikaein2014oai,gomez2016srslte}. One constraint shapes everything that follows. E2 carries aggregated measurements, not packets, so a flow-level detector needs a data path of its own (Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice}).

\subsection{Security of Open RAN}

The RIC and its third-party xApps are themselves a new attack surface, according to the O-RAN Alliance threat model~\cite{etsi_wg11_threat} and several independent analyses~\cite{liyanage2023openran,mimran2022security}, and security mechanisms for the O-RAN interfaces have since been built and evaluated~\cite{groen2025implementing}. On the detection side, 5G-Spector offers layer-3 attack detection as an O-RAN compliant service~\cite{wen2024spector}, Det-RAN detects attacks from cross-layer data in real time~\cite{scalingi2024detran}, and Fard et al. fuse radio telemetry and flow records on the same source corpus we use~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}. All of them build or test a detector inside one deployment. Our question is what evidence would show that such a detector still works in another.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Network Intrusion Detection}

Public intrusion corpora are plentiful for IoT traffic~\cite{koroniotis2019botiot,moustafa2021toniot,ferrag2022edgeiiot,neto2023ciciot,sharafaldin2018cicids} and scarce for cellular networks. 5G-NIDD was captured on an operational 5G test network~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, while NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD pairs flow records with radio telemetry from an O-RAN testbed~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}. On 5G-NIDD, the dataset authors report binary accuracies of 99.85\% to 99.95\% for four of five classifiers on a random 70/30 split~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv}, and later work reaches a weighted $F_1$ of up to 99.95\%~\cite{ilias2024moe5g}. Standardized NetFlow feature sets made cross-corpus experiments possible~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}. The corpora can be wrong, however. Engelen et al. documented labeling and flow-construction faults in CIC-IDS2017~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}, and Flood et al. found design flaws across NIDS benchmarks that let a classifier score well without learning the attack~\cite{flood2024smells}.

\subsection{Evaluation Practice and Dataset Shift}

Reported results tend to overstate what a detector can do, and the causes are well known: closed-world evaluation~\cite{sommer2010outside}, neglect of the base rate~\cite{axelsson2000baserate}, sampling bias and data leakage~\cite{arp2022dosdonts,kapoor2023leakage}, and temporal or spatial bias in the split~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}. Apruzzese et al. trace the gap between reported and operational effectiveness across security domains~\cite{apruzzese2023role}, Kus et al. see the same gap in industrial intrusion detection once detectors meet data they were not tuned on~\cite{kus2022falsesense}, and cross-dataset generalization has repeatedly proved poor~\cite{dhooge2020interdataset,layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. Closest to our work, Abraheem and Edhirig transfer XGBoost between the same two corpora with 15 harmonized features and report zero-shot balanced accuracies per attack family of 0.50 to 0.56 from NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD to 5G-NIDD and 0.52 to 0.83 in the other direction~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}. Our transfer results match theirs in direction and size. We add the protocol ladder with input-blind baselines, references trained inside the target, the published pipeline under the same ladder, an audit of the target labels, and the operational and latency criteria. From the dataset-shift literature we take label-shift estimation from unlabeled data~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,lipton2018bbse}, calibration and its behavior under shift~\cite{guo2017calibration,ovadia2019trust,fawcett2007pav}, shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing}, and covariance alignment for domain adaptation~\cite{sun2016coral}. Classifiers compared under repeated resampling are tested as Nadeau and Bengio recommend~\cite{nadeau2003inference}.

\section{Deployment Criteria and Threat Model}
\label{sec:criteria}

We state the system and threat model, define the three quantities we measure, and combine them into a deployability test.

\subsection{System and Threat Model}

A near-real-time RIC serves a set of E2 nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), and user devices, IoT devices among them, attach to those nodes. The adversary controls some of these devices and uses them for flooding, scanning, brute-force login, or web attacks, the categories present in our corpora. It does not control the RIC, the E2 interface, or the xApp. The defender's xApp reads either radio telemetry windows delivered through E2SM-KPM or flow records from an exporter placed with the user plane, and it can act through E2SM-RC. That exporter has to live outside the RIC, since E2 carries no packets. Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice} returns to its cost and placement.

\subsection{Criterion 1: Generalization Across Deployments}

Let \DA{} and \DB{} be two independently collected corpora in a shared feature space. We describe a model $f_{\theta}$ trained on \DA{} by how far its score on \DB{} falls below its held-out score on \DA{}. The two corpora differ in attack prevalence (Table~\ref{tab:corpora}), which complicates the choice of metric. A change in macro-$F_1$ blends a change in the classifier with a change in class balance, and even a classifier that ignores its input scores differently when the prevalence moves. We therefore use the difference in balanced accuracy, which prevalence does not affect,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{BA}} = \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\mathrm{test}}\right) - \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{B}\right),
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
and we also give the macro-$F_1$ gap $\Delta_{F_1}$ and its difference from the gap of the majority-class baseline. Covariate shift is measured one feature at a time,
\begin{equation}
\bar{W}_{1} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} W_{1}\!\left(Q_{j}(P_{A}^{(j)}),\, Q_{j}(P_{B}^{(j)})\right),
\label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{j}$ is the quantile transform of feature $j$, fitted on source training data only. Had each corpus been normalized by its own quantiles, both marginals would become uniform and every term would vanish.

\subsection{Criterion 2: Alert Burden Under a Realistic Base Rate}

Let $\lambda_{b}$ be the benign arrival rate in the detector's own unit, which is flow records per hour for a flow-level detector and telemetry windows per hour for a window-level one. A benign UE produces 225 windows of 16\,s per hour. False alerts then arrive at
\begin{equation}
A(\tau) = \lambda_{b}\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau),
\label{eq:alerts}
\end{equation}
and, for an operational attack prevalence $\pi$, the fraction of alerts that correspond to attacks is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PPV}(\tau) = \frac{\pi R(\tau)}{\pi R(\tau) + (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)}.
\label{eq:ppv}
\end{equation}
The corpora cannot give us $\pi$ or $\lambda_{b}$, so we declare both and vary them over a range. $\mathrm{PPV}$ depends only on $\pi$, and $A$ grows linearly with $\lambda_{b}$. For that reason we also report false alerts per $10^{6}$ benign flows, a number that does not depend on $\lambda_b$.

\subsection{Criterion 3: Latency Against the Control Budget}

The decision latency of an xApp can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}
L = t_{\mathrm{ind}} + t_{\mathrm{feat}} + t_{\mathrm{inf}} + t_{\mathrm{act}} + t_{q},
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
where the terms are the delivery of the indication to the xApp, feature construction, inference, the control action up to its acknowledgment, and queueing inside the xApp. We time feature construction and inference alone on one host, and all five terms in a loop through a near-real-time RIC with an emulated E2 node (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). What matters for conformance is the tail. For a budget $B$ and a tolerated violation rate $\varepsilon$, we require $q_{1-\varepsilon}(L) \le B$. The O-RAN E2 specification allows near-real-time latency requirements anywhere from 10\,ms to 1\,s~\cite{etsi_e2gap}, so instead of a verdict for one chosen value we report the smallest budget that is met.

\subsection{A Deployability Test}

The three criteria are combined into a test that a detector either passes or fails:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{P}(f_{\theta}) = {} & \ind\!\left[\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}} \le \delta\right] \cdot \ind\!\left[\overline{q}_{0.99}(L) \le B\right] \\
& \cdot \ind\!\big[\exists\,\tau:\ R(\tau)\ge r_{\min},\ A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \\
& \qquad\quad \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho\big],
\end{split}
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}}$ and $\overline{q}_{0.99}$ are upper 95\% confidence bounds, so a pass cannot hinge on a lucky point estimate, and every alert condition is evaluated on the target corpus. The minimum recall $r_{\min}$ keeps a detector that never raises an alert from passing the alert term. As operating threshold we take the most sensitive admissible one,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\tau^{\star} = \arg\max_{\tau} R(\tau) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad & R(\tau) \ge r_{\min},\ A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \\
& \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho .
\end{split}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
We intend this test as a reporting instrument, not as an O-RAN requirement. Its bounds stand for operator requirements. Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate} gives the values we used and shows how the verdict responds when they change.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Corpora}

\DA{} is NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}, recorded on a physical OpenAirInterface O-RAN testbed. It holds benign traffic and five attack categories: denial of service (DoS), distributed denial of service (DDoS), probing, brute force, and web attacks. The corpus has two layers, and the published files do not let us join them record by record. One is flow records exported by Zeek at the central unit. The other is radio telemetry sampled at 1\,Hz at the distributed unit. We use both. For the radio layer we form windows of 16 consecutive records of one UE and summarize each by the mean and standard deviation of 16 KPM fields.

Gaps of more than five minutes in the timestamps split the radio layer into 30 capture sessions. Fard et al. describe the same corpus as \SessRunN{} experiment runs~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}, and a gap threshold of \SessRunGapMin{} to \SessRunGapMax{}\,s does recover exactly \SessRunN{} segments, each with a single attack category. Every one of our sessions is a union of complete segments. The five-minute rule merges runs recorded back to back (\SessMultiSub{} of the 30 sessions contain two or three attack subcategories), which keeps such runs on the same side of any split. All records of a session carry the same category label. A radio label is therefore a session label, and every window of an attack session is an attack window. The sessions follow the capture schedule (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}): nine benign sessions on days 0 to 6, the attack categories in blocks on days 47 to 53, and one last benign session.

\DB{} is 5G-NIDD~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, captured on a 5G test network at another institution and exported by Argus, with two attack categories (DoS and probing). Its combined files have no addresses, ports, or timestamps. They do keep the Argus record counter and file offset, and because the offset restarts at every file boundary we can recover 20 capture files, two passes over the same ten attack sessions, one per base station. The row index of \texttt{Combined.csv} also restarts at the second pass. Each attack file holds benign traffic plus one attack type. A second release of the corpus keeps every Argus field, addresses included, and the raw captures are public as well (Sections~\ref{sec:res:target} and~\ref{sec:res:anatomy}).

A third corpus, \DC{}, gives us a second transfer target at a third site. It contains the flow records of a 5G core testbed (Open5GS in Docker) published by Nugraha et al.~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}. Only three of its files hold flow records, each with one attack (a SYN flood, an ICMP flood, and a PFCP session-deletion attack), for \ThNflows{} flows in total, \ThNattack{} of them attacks. Its benign flows mix user traffic with the signaling of the core network. NFStream exported \DC{}, and it counts link-layer bytes plus, for flows inside the GTP-U user-plane tunnel, 44 bytes of encapsulation per packet. We strip both overheads so that the shared columns hold the IP bytes of the inner packet, as they do for \DA{}. Table~\ref{tab:corpora} summarizes the three datasets.

\subsection{Shared Feature Space}

Zeek and Argus share no column names, so we define the flow feature space by what each field means (Table~\ref{tab:features}). It has 15 concepts in 18 columns: seven counts that both exporters report, a one-hot protocol with a fixed list of levels, and seven derived quantities computed the same way for both corpora. One mapping is easy to get wrong. Zeek's \texttt{src\_bytes} counts application payload, which is zero for most floods and scans, whereas Argus's \texttt{SrcBytes} counts whole packets. The true counterpart of \texttt{SrcBytes} is Zeek's \texttt{src\_ip\_bytes}, at 40 and 42 bytes per packet, respectively. Both corpora get the same lower bound on duration and the same $\log(1+x)$ transform for unbounded quantities, and scaling is fitted on source training data alone. No address, port, or timestamp is ever a feature.

Before computing any result with it, we also fixed an exporter-robust subset of nine columns: the protocol and the five per-packet means and direction ratios. The two exporters end flows at different points, which moves duration, rates, and totals. Per-packet means and direction ratios move less, although they are not invariant. Splitting a flow changes them whenever its packets differ (handshake and teardown packets landing in different records, for example), and the exporters count the same packet as 40 and 42 bytes, an offset of about 5\% for minimum-size packets.

\subsection{Detectors}

Every protocol is run on six supervised architectures and two trivial baselines. The supervised models are LR, a DT of depth 12, an RF~\cite{breiman2001random}, XGB~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, the HGB implementation of scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, and an MLP. The baselines are a majority-class predictor and a stratified sampler of the training prior. They ignore their input, so any change in their score between two protocols comes from class composition alone. All supervised models use balanced class weights (sample weights for the MLP, \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} for XGB). We fixed the hyperparameters and did not tune them (Table~\ref{tab:hyper}). Tuning on held-out source data would reward source-specific structure, which is exactly what the transfer experiments measure, and tuning on the target would leak target information into training.

Three more model families answer narrower questions. Two sequence models, a GRU~\cite{cho2014gru} and a one-dimensional convolutional network, read the raw 16-step KPM sequences of the same radio windows. Two unsupervised detectors, an isolation forest~\cite{liu2008isolation} and an autoencoder, learn from benign source flows only, and their alarm threshold sits at the 99th percentile of benign source scores. Finally, a CORAL variant~\cite{sun2016coral} re-colors the source flows to the covariance of the unlabeled target before training. It is the one case in which a detector sees target data.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}

Splits are group-disjoint wherever a group key exists, and an automated check rejects any split that puts a group on both sides. On the radio layer the group key is the capture session. We compare three protocols there: a random split of windows, a run-disjoint split, and a category-stratified run-disjoint split that keeps sessions of every attack category on both sides, so that no test category is missing from training. Two further designs probe the effect of time (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}). One holds out the latest or the earliest session of every category and compares the outcome with draws that hold out a random session per category. The other holds out each benign session on its own. On the flow layer the group key is the source address (318 groups). For each of 20 split seeds we draw 300,000 flows from \DA{}, divide them by source address, and evaluate every model on the whole of \DB{}. The reverse direction trains on a random split of \DB{} and tests on \DA{}. Inside \DB{}, the in-target references either hold out four of the 20 capture files or train on one base station's pass and test on the other's (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}). Apart from these references, CORAL, the domain classifier, and the prior estimates of Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration} (all marked as such), no transform, threshold, or model choice touches \DB{}. Every access to it is logged.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

We estimate uncertainty over 20 split seeds. The splits resample one population of 30 sessions or 318 source addresses, so their training and test sets overlap, and a paired $t$-test over split means understates the variance. Our intervals are therefore Nadeau--Bengio corrected~\cite{nadeau2003inference}: the variance is scaled by $1/J + n_{\mathrm{test}}/n_{\mathrm{train}}$ for $J$ splits. Holm correction is applied within each family of tests, where a family is one metric and one comparison (a protocol pair or a transfer direction) across the six supervised architectures. The two sequence models form a family of their own. Operating points come from confusion counts pooled over folds, for reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}. A pooled count includes a sample once for every seed in which it is tested, and samples in the same cluster are correlated. A binomial interval would be far too narrow. We use a cluster bootstrap with 2,000 replicates instead, resampling with replacement both the split seeds and the clusters (radio sessions, \DA{} source addresses, or \DB{} capture files).

\subsection{Experimental Pipeline and Inference Loop}

Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the experimental pipeline, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:xapp} the decision loop of a detection xApp. A decision that misses the budget is still acted on and logged as a violation, because a late mitigation is usually better than none. It scores held-out test windows in place of windows built from the synthetic KPM values, and it sends a control message after every report, whatever the score, so that $t_{\mathrm{act}}$ is measured each time.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All measurements ran on one workstation: a 13th Gen Intel Core i9-13900H processor (14 cores, 20 threads), 47.6\,GB of memory, and Windows 11 Pro build 26120, with Python 3.11.9, scikit-learn 1.8.0, XGBoost 3.2.0, NumPy 2.3.5, and ONNX Runtime 1.30.0. The sequence models were trained with PyTorch 2.14 on the CPU in a separate environment. The full package list ships with the results.

For the latency loop we built FlexRIC~\cite{schmidt2021flexric} v2.0.0 in WSL2 (Ubuntu, Linux kernel 6.18) on the same machine and connected its near-real-time RIC over SCTP to the emulated gNB agent that comes with FlexRIC. Our xApp is written in C. It subscribes to E2SM-KPM reports with 16 measurement items per UE, the size of one radio record, scores every UE in a report with ONNX Runtime 1.30.0 through its C API, and answers each report with one E2SM-RC control message (radio bearer control, QoS flow mapping, the only control the emulated node implements). Then it waits for the acknowledgment. Because the emulated node reports synthetic values for 0 to \RicUEsMax{} UEs, the xApp scores held-out test windows in their place. Before any timing, the C code reproduced the Python scores on all 589 test windows to within $4\times10^{-7}$. Delivery time comes from the timestamp the node writes into the KPM header, which works because all processes share one clock. No radio, no real E2 node, and no container platform took part. All other latency figures were measured without a RIC.

The protocol comparison on the radio layer uses 20 split seeds with two model seeds each, and the other radio-layer and flow-layer experiments use 20 split seeds with one model seed. Calibration uses 10 split seeds of a three-way source split into training, calibration, and test groups. We declare an attack prevalence of $\pi = 0.002$ and a benign arrival rate of $\lambda_{b} = 240{,}000$ flows per hour and vary both over a range. On the radio layer, false alerts are counted per benign UE-hour. Neither parameter comes from an operator. Our rate of about 67 flows per second is below the several hundred flows per second that Jin et al. expect at a heavily loaded base station of a large LTE network~\cite{jin2013softcell}, so the alert volumes we report probably understate those of an edge site serving several cells. Latency is measured per call with a monotonic clock and garbage collection enabled, after 1,000 warm-up calls, over 20,000 calls, with one thread per library. Every quantile comes with a distribution-free confidence interval.

\subsection{Evaluation-Protocol Sensitivity}
\label{sec:res:protocol}

Table~\ref{tab:leakage} and Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage} compare three protocols on the radio layer. Against a run-disjoint split, a random split raises macro-$F_1$ by \LeakMin{} to \LeakMax{} across the six architectures, \LeakAvg{} on average, and the gain is positive in at least \LeakSeedsMin{} of the 20 splits for every architecture. The trivial baselines gain nothing. For the average gain, the corrected interval is [\LeakAvgNbLo, \LeakAvgNbHi] ($p = \LeakAvgPnb$). With only thirty capture sessions we cannot pin down the size of the effect, and no single architecture's gain survives Holm correction. An uncorrected paired $t$-test on the same data would have given $p = \LeakAvgPt$.

The gain is not caused by test categories missing from training: in all 20 run-disjoint splits, every test category also appeared in training. The category-stratified protocol guarantees this by design and gives a gain of \LeakStratMin{} to \LeakStratMax{}. We do not count its larger gain as extra evidence, though, because it pushes sessions of rare categories into the test set and makes that set harder. A model-free probe exposes the mechanism. Under a random split, \NNSame\% of the test windows have their nearest training window in the same capture session (\NNSameMin\% to \NNSameMax\% across splits), and a one-nearest-neighbor (1-NN) lookup reaches a macro-$F_1$ of \NNoneRandom{}, against \NNoneRun{} under a run-disjoint split (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). Since every radio label is also a session label, knowing the session is enough to know the label. A random split lets any model use this, a lookup table included.

Under the run-disjoint split, the lookup beats LR and DT and comes within \NNgapBest{} of the best supervised architecture. On this layer, then, the learned models add little to simply retrieving similar windows. The two sequence models in Table~\ref{tab:sequence}, which read the raw 16-step KPM sequences instead of window summaries, show the same pattern more sharply. They reach \SeqRandomMin{} to \SeqRandomMax{} under a random split and \SeqRDmin{} to \SeqRDmax{} under a run-disjoint split. That gain of \SeqGainMin{} to \SeqGainMax{} has corrected intervals that exclude zero for both models (Holm-adjusted $p \le \SeqPholmMax{}$ within the pair).

\subsection{Time Order and Benign Sessions}
\label{sec:res:drift}

The radio layer spans \DriftDays{} days, but its capture order follows the scenario schedule (Table~\ref{tab:benign}). Nine benign sessions were recorded on days 0 to 6, the five attack categories in blocks on days 47 to 53, and a last benign session on day 53. Each session is benign or belongs to one attack category from start to end. Split the sessions by order, and whole attack categories end up held out. For training fractions between 0.4 and 0.7, the latest sessions contain \DvAuditUnseenMin{} to \DvAuditUnseenMax{} attack categories never seen in training, plus a single benign session. What such a split measures is the detection of unseen categories. It is not temporal drift, and we do not report it as a drift estimate.

To hold category coverage fixed, we held out the latest session of every category, the benign category included, and compared the result with \DvNctrl{} control draws that each hold out a randomly chosen session per category (Table~\ref{tab:timesplit}). Holding out the latest sessions gives a balanced accuracy of \DvFwdBAmin{} to \DvFwdBAmax{}, against control means of \DvCtrlBAmin{} to \DvCtrlBAmax{}. Yet \DvFwdPctMin\% to \DvFwdPctMax\% of the control draws score just as low. Holding out the earliest sessions gives \DvRevBAmin{} to \DvRevBAmax{}, a level that \DvRevPctMin\% to \DvRevPctMax\% of the control draws match or beat. Time order, in short, stays within the ordinary variation between sessions.

Much of that variation depends on which benign session ends up in the test set. Held out one at a time (Table~\ref{tab:benign} and Fig.~\ref{fig:benign}), \BenLowN{} of the ten benign sessions give a false positive rate of at most \BenLowMax{}, while sessions \BenHighSessions{} (days \BenHighDays{}) give between \BenHighMin{} and \BenHighMax{}. The last benign session, the only one recorded during the attack period, reaches \BenLateFPR{} (\BenLateFA{} false alerts per benign UE-hour). Two sessions from the first week come close to that. With ten benign sessions, a change over time cannot be told apart from this heterogeneity, and a detector's false positive rate at a new site cannot be predicted from its rate on the benign sessions it was tested on.

\subsection{The Target Corpus: Labels and a Published Result}
\label{sec:res:target}

\emph{Label conflicts.} Before running any transfer to \DB{}, we checked whether its labels follow from its flow records. For half of the corpus they do not. Of the \NDbFlows{} flows, \LcShareAll\% (\LcFlowsAll) match a flow with the opposite label in every field except the row index, \texttt{Seq}, and \texttt{Offset} (Table~\ref{tab:conflict}, Fig.~\ref{fig:conflict}). All of them sit in the two UDP-flood captures, one per base station, where they make up \LcBenignFileConf\% of file~\LcBenignFile{} and \LcAttackFileConf\% of file~\LcAttackFile{}. The match is exact. Each of the \LcTwinAttack{} flows labeled UDP flood in file~\LcAttackFile{} has an identical copy labeled benign in file~\LcBenignFile{}, and for \LcTwinEqual{} of the \LcTwinRecords{} distinct records the two counts are equal. The copies amount to \LcBenignTwinPct\% of the corpus's benign class.

Which label is right? The combined files carry no addresses, but the release with all fields preserved does, and it lines up with them row for row. All \LcTwinBenign{} benign copies are UDP flows from \LcAddrSrc{} to \LcAddrDst{}. That host pair, protocol, and time window make up the flood labeled as attack in file~\LcAttackFile{}. The attack labeled in file~\LcBenignFile{} comes from a different host, \LcAddrOwnSrc{}, aimed at the same address. The capture at the first station evidently recorded the second station's flood too, and only its own attacker got an attack label. We therefore treat the benign copies as mislabeled attack traffic, which makes the evaluation without them the one with correct labels. No label is changed, and every \DB{} result is reported with and without the copies.

These conflicts put a ceiling on any classifier that reads the record content. Even evaluated on the very flows it was built from, the best possible function of the \LcNFeat{} content fields reaches a balanced accuracy of only \LcCeilNative{}. In the 18 shared columns the ceiling is \LcCeilShared{}, which is where DT, XGB, HGB, and the MLP land when trained and tested on a random split of \DB{} (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}). Add \texttt{Seq} and \texttt{Offset}, and every record becomes unique. The ceiling then rises to \LcCeilPos{}.

\emph{References within the target.} To tell the cost of transfer apart from the limits of \DB{} itself, we also trained and tested the six architectures on \DB{} alone (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}). A random split gives \TgtRandShBAmin{} to \TgtRandShBAmax{} in the shared space, which is the ceiling above, and \TgtRandShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtRandShCleanBAmax{} once the benign copies leave the test set. Held-out capture files give \TgtFileShBAmin{} to \TgtFileShBAmax{} in the shared space and \TgtFileNatBAmin{} to \TgtFileNatBAmax{} with native features, although none of the three draws held out a UDP-flood capture. Moving between base stations, inside one corpus and with one exporter, drops balanced accuracy to \TgtSiteABShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteABShBAmax{} from station 1 to station 2 and to \TgtSiteBAShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShBAmax{} the other way. The copies cause both drops. Trained on station 1, where the flood copies carry benign labels, a model misses the floods of station 2. Trained on station 2, it flags the benign-labeled copies of station 1, and it scores \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmax{} when those copies are removed from the test set. Where labels follow from record content, the shared space separates the classes of \DB{} almost perfectly. The target corpus is not what limits transfer.

\subsection{Transfer Across Corpora}
\label{sec:res:transfer}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer} give the results of transfer from \DA{} to \DB{}. Measured in macro-$F_1$, every supervised model loses \TrFoneDropMin{} to \TrFoneDropMax{}. So does the majority baseline, which uses no features at all: it loses \TrMajDrop{}, simply because \DB{} has a lower attack prevalence than \DA{}. Net of that baseline, the macro-$F_1$ loss is \TrDiDmin{} to \TrDiDmax{}, and none of these losses stays significant after correction. Macro-$F_1$ is the wrong yardstick here. Balanced accuracy, which prevalence leaves untouched, shows the loss plainly. It falls from \TrSrcBAmin{}--\TrSrcBAmax{} on held-out source data to \TrTgtBAmin{}--\TrTgtBAmax{} on \DB{}. This drop of \TrBAdropMin{} to \TrBAdropMax{} is significant for \TrSigBA{} of the six architectures after correction, positive in at least \TrBAseedsMin{} of the 20 splits for every architecture, and \TrBAdropAvg{} [\TrBAdropAvgNbLo, \TrBAdropAvgNbHi] on average. The trivial baselines do not move, and target ROC-AUC lies between \TrTgtAUCmin{} and \TrTgtAUCmax{}.

The detectors stay above chance on the target, but not by much. Their average target balanced accuracy is \TrTgtBAavg{}, and the corrected interval excludes 0.5 for \TrChanceSig{} of the six architectures (\TrChanceSigHolm{} after Holm correction). As floors, the stratified sampler scores a macro-$F_1$ of \TrStratFloor{} on \DB{} and a fair coin would score \TrFairCoin{}. We give both, because a macro-$F_1$ floor depends on the prior it samples from. On every prevalence-independent measure LR transfers best (balanced accuracy \TrLRtgtBA{}, ROC-AUC \TrLRtgtAUC{}). The MLP, strongest on held-out source data (\TrMLPsrcBA{}), reaches \TrMLPtgtBA{} on the target. Source performance, in other words, does not predict target performance in a prevalence-independent metric: the rank correlation in balanced accuracy across the six architectures is \RhoBA{} ($p=\RhoBAP$). In macro-$F_1$ it is \RhoFone{} ($p=\RhoFoneP$), but macro-$F_1$ rankings move with prevalence, and six architectures give little precision to either estimate.

\emph{Effect of the target labels.} Some of this loss comes from the label conflict of Section~\ref{sec:res:target}. A detector that learned to recognize a UDP flood on \DA{} finds the floods in capture file~\LcAttackFile{} and, by the same rule, flags \NetCopyFlagMin{} to \NetCopyFlagMax{} of the benign-labeled copies in file~\LcBenignFile{}. LR finds neither. With the copies excluded, in a deterministic re-run of 10 of the 20 split seeds, target balanced accuracy is \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{} (\TrCleanOthMin{} to \TrCleanOthMax{} for the five nonlinear architectures, \TrCleanLR{} for LR). The loss against held-out source data shrinks to \TrCleanDropMin{} to \TrCleanDropMax{} and is significant for \TrCleanSig{} of the six architectures after correction. The conflicts turn the ranking upside down. LR looks like the best transfer model on all flows and is the worst on the flows with consistent labels. Even on these flows, transfer falls \TrCleanShortMin{} to \TrCleanShortMax{} short of the in-target reference in balanced accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}).

\subsection{Composition of the Transfer Gap}
\label{sec:res:anatomy}

\emph{Separability.} A classifier trained to tell \DA{} flows from \DB{} flows reaches a balanced accuracy of \DomShared{} on the 18 shared columns and \DomRobust{} on the nine exporter-robust columns alone. The largest per-feature shifts are in the protocol mix (most \DA{} flows are TCP, most \DB{} flows UDP), the direction ratios, and the packet counts (Fig.~\ref{fig:shift}). The robust columns shift as much as the segmentation-dependent ones ($\bar{W}_1$ of \WoneRobust{} against \WoneNonrobust). So the corpora differ both in flow segmentation, which a common exporter would remove, and in traffic composition, which no exporter would.

\emph{A common exporter.} Because the raw captures of \DB{} are public, we could test the exporter directly. We re-extracted them with Zeek \ZkVersion{}, the exporter of \DA{}, and mapped the Zeek records into the shared space with the same function used for \DA{}. In every capture of \DB{}, the published attack flows belong to one host pair. Labeling a flow as attack when its host pair is an attack pair of the same capture reproduces \ZkRuleAgreePct\% of the published labels, so we carried those labels over to the \ZkFlows{} Zeek flows (\ZkAttackPct\% attacks). The flood copies of Section~\ref{sec:res:target} reappear in the Zeek output (\ZkCopies{} flows). They are in the captures, not an artifact of the Argus export. We then reran the transfer on the same \ZkSeeds{} split seeds as the Argus results without the copies. The trained models are identical, and only the export of the target changes.

The gap does not close. On all flows, balanced accuracy falls from \ZkArgBAmin{}--\ZkArgBAmax{} with Argus to \ZkBAmin{}--\ZkBAmax{} with Zeek, and the paired difference over seeds (\ZkDiffMin{} to \ZkDiffMax{}) has a 95\% interval below zero for \ZkSigNeg{} of the six architectures. Without the copies, five architectures move by \ZkCleanOthDiffMin{} to \ZkCleanOthDiffMax{}, none significantly, and LR loses \ZkCleanLRloss{}. Under Zeek the false positive rate rises for every architecture, to \ZkCleanFPRmin{}--\ZkCleanFPRmax{} without the copies against \ZkCleanArgFPRmin{}--\ZkCleanArgFPRmax{} with Argus. What is left is the difference in traffic composition that the domain classifier and the robust columns had already pointed to.

\emph{Model families and adaptation.} Table~\ref{tab:arms} runs the same splits under three modifications, and none of them produces a detector that transfers. Keeping only the nine exporter-robust columns lifts held-out source balanced accuracy to \RobSrcBAmin{} to \RobSrcBAmax{}, but target balanced accuracy drops to \RobBAmin{} to \RobBAmax{}, no better than chance. The columns that do not depend on flow segmentation carry the compositional shift. CORAL, which reads unlabeled target features, gives a target balanced accuracy of \CoralBAmin{} to \CoralBAmax{}. It helps only the MLP (\CoralMLPtgtBA{} against \TrMLPtgtBA{} without adaptation), and it pushes the target false positive rate up to \CoralFPRmin{} to \CoralFPRmax{}. The novelty detectors, trained on benign traffic alone, already fail on held-out source data, because in this feature space floods and scans look like short benign flows.

\emph{Cost of the shared space.} Projecting \DA{} onto the 18 shared columns costs \HarmBAmin{} to \HarmBAmax{} of in-distribution balanced accuracy compared with all \NFullFeat{} non-port Zeek features (Table~\ref{tab:harm}). The low source macro-$F_1$ values in Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reflect the \PrevDa\% attack prevalence of \DA{}, not weak detectors, since the matching source balanced accuracy is \TrSrcBAmin{} to \TrSrcBAmax.

\subsection{A Third Corpus}
\label{sec:res:third}

Do these results hold beyond one pair of corpora? To find out, we added \DC{}, the flow records of a 5G core testbed~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}, as a second target. It changes the site, the flow exporter, and the vantage point together, and unlike \DB{} its labels are consistent. The false positive rate is \ThAFPRmin{} to \ThAFPRmax{}, and up to \ThABgFlagMax{} of the core network's own signaling is flagged. The loss against held-out source data (\ThADropMin{} to \ThADropMax{}) varies so much between split seeds that none of it is significant after correction. \subsection{Alert Burden}
\label{sec:res:alerts}

Table~\ref{tab:pooled} gives pooled operating points at $\tau=0.5$ for three evaluation sets, with 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals. On the radio layer, the six detectors have a pooled window-level false positive rate of \RadFPRmin{} to \RadFPRmax{} (\RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} false alerts per benign UE-hour) and, at $\pi=0.002$, an operational precision of \RadPPVmin{} to \RadPPVmax{}, against a corpus precision of \RadPrecMin{} to \RadPrecMax{}. These point estimates are unstable. The false positive rate depends on which of the ten benign sessions enter the test set (Table~\ref{tab:benign}). The bootstrap interval of the pooled rate spans \RadCbFPRlo{} to \RadCbFPRhi{} across architectures (\RadCbFAlo{} to \RadCbFAhi{} false alerts per benign UE-hour), and the interval for operational precision at $\tau=0.5$ spans \RadCbPPVlo{} to \RadCbPPVhi{}. Thirty sessions cannot fix the alert burden of the radio layer. All they show is that it depends on the benign conditions a detector happens to meet.

The flow layer tells a similar story. On held-out \DA{} flows the false positive rate is \NetSrcFPRmin{} to \NetSrcFPRmax{}, which at $\lambda_b = 240{,}000$ benign flows per hour means \NetSrcFAmin{} to \NetSrcFAmax{} false alerts per hour. On \DB{} it is \NetTgtFPRmin{} to \NetTgtFPRmax{}, or \NetTgtFAmin{} to \NetTgtFAmax{} false alerts per hour. Without the benign copies of Section~\ref{sec:res:target} it is \NetCleanFPRmin{} to \NetCleanFPRmax{}. That still comes to \NetCleanFAmin{} to \NetCleanFAmax{} false alerts per hour, at an operational precision of \NetCleanPPVmin{} to \NetCleanPPVmax{}.

Raising the decision threshold improves precision only on held-out \DA{} flows (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}). Because the best threshold is picked on the evaluation data, the following values are upper bounds. With recall kept above 10\%, high thresholds reach an operational precision of \NetSrcPPVbest{} on held-out \DA{} flows. On \DB{}, the best any detector achieves is \NetTgtPPVbest{}, or \NetCleanPPVbest{} without the copies. The radio layer's best pooled value is \RadPPVbest{}, but for \RadCbBestHiN{} of the six architectures its bootstrap interval reaches 1.0: a resample that leaves out the benign sessions flagged almost entirely leaves thresholds with no false positive at all. How corpus precision relates to operational precision is pure arithmetic in $\pi$. Moving $\pi$ from $10^{-4}$ to 0.5 changes the ratio between them from \RadCollapseLowPi{} to \RadCollapseHighPi{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:prevalence}). An operational precision means something only when the assumed prevalence is stated.
\emph{Estimation of operational precision.} Near zero false positives, PPV is strongly nonlinear, so a fold that happens to contain no false positive yields $\mathrm{PPV}=1$ whatever its recall. Averaging PPV over folds rewards the architecture that happens to draw the most such folds. On the radio layer, the same counts put the ratio between the best and the worst architecture at \EstPooled{} when pooled, \EstMedian{} as a median over folds, and \EstMean{} as a mean over folds (Table~\ref{tab:estimator}). We use pooled counts throughout.

\subsection{Calibration and Prior Shift}
\label{sec:res:calibration}

Under transfer the scores are poorly calibrated (Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}). We fitted three calibrators on a group-disjoint calibration fold from the source (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). Whether a calibrator can move the achievable operating points depends on whether it keeps the order of the scores. Temperature scaling does, and it changes ROC-AUC by at most \CalTempMax{}. Platt scaling is monotone too, but its fitted slope can be negative, and for DT it inverted the ranking in \CalPlattFlipSeeds{} of \CalSeeds{} seeds, shifting ROC-AUC by \CalPlattFlip. In that seed the tree's scores carried no ranking information on the calibration fold (ROC-AUC \PlattCalAUC{} on \PlattCalGroups{} held-out source addresses) even though they ranked the test fold well (\PlattTestAUC). Platt scaling fitted the calibration fold correctly, with a slope of \PlattSlope{}, and in doing so inverted the ranking on the test fold. Small group-disjoint calibration folds, which need not resemble the test fold, are to blame here, not Platt scaling. Isotonic regression is monotone but piecewise constant. It merges scores into ties and, on its fitting data, yields the ROC convex hull~\cite{fawcett2007pav}, and its largest change in ROC-AUC ranged from \CalIsoMin{} to \CalIsoMax{} across architectures. No calibrator lifts the target macro-$F_1$ above that of the raw scores for any architecture. Fitted on a source with \PrevDa\% attacks, they pull the scores toward that prior and flag more benign traffic on \DB{}.

The usual remedy for prior shift is to rescale the scores to the target prior~\cite{saerens2002adjusting}, which a deployment does not know. Expectation maximization~\cite{saerens2002adjusting} and black-box shift estimation~\cite{lipton2018bbse} estimate it from unlabeled data under the assumption that only the class balance changes. That assumption fails here. For a true target prior of \PriorTrue{}, the mean estimates per architecture run from \PriorMeanMin{} to \PriorMeanMax{}, and \PriorFracExtreme\% of the individual estimates fall below 0.05 or above 0.95. Even an oracle, rescaling with the true prior, does not beat the raw scores (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). The detectors are limited by discrimination on the target (ROC-AUC \TrTgtAUCmin{} to \TrTgtAUCmax{}), not by calibration.

\subsection{Implications for O-RAN Practice}
\label{sec:disc:practice}

\emph{Data path.} E2SM-KPM delivers aggregated measurements once per reporting period. It carries no packets. A window-level detector can live inside the RIC and work on KPM reports. A flow-level detector cannot: it needs a flow exporter near the user plane, in the central unit or at the user-plane function, plus some way to move its records to the xApp, such as a custom E2 service model or the non-real-time data collection path. That transport adds to $t_{\mathrm{ind}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:latency}. Our RIC loop measures $t_{\mathrm{ind}}$ for KPM reports only and says nothing about the flow layer.

\emph{Detection delay.} Computation is a small part of the time it takes to detect an attack. A window-level decision has to wait for the end of its window (16\,s here). A flow-level decision has to wait until the flow record is exported, which for a long connection means waiting for an idle or active timeout. Anyone reporting computational latency should report the KPM reporting period and the flow timeouts alongside it.

\emph{Acting on alerts.} An E2SM-RC action such as isolating or steering a UE hits a falsely accused UE just as hard as a guilty one. At the false positive rates in Table~\ref{tab:pooled}, automatic mitigation on the radio layer would act against every benign UE \RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} times an hour. Alerts of this quality can inform an operator, or a non-real-time policy through A1. They should not drive automatic control.

\emph{Admission and monitoring.} The deployability test of Eq.~\eqref{eq:deploy} could serve as an admission check before an xApp is switched on at a new site, provided labeled benign and attack traffic from that site is available. After admission, unsupervised shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing} can flag the moment incoming traffic departs from the training distribution. The corpora we studied can be told apart with a balanced accuracy of \DomShared{}, so a monitor of this kind would fire at once.

\subsection{Relation to Reported Results}

Most published results for O-RAN and 5G intrusion detection are in-distribution scores on corpora other than ours, and under a random split our own in-distribution scores are just as high (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). \section{Reproducibility and Ethics}
\label{sec:repro}

The code, the configuration, the seeded split procedures (every split can be regenerated deterministically), the raw per-seed results, and the generators for every table, figure, and in-text number are available at \url{https://github.com/adeliusa486/oran-ids-deployability}. One script regenerates every number, table, and figure of this paper from the per-seed results and checks them against the manuscript. The same script can re-run each experiment from the raw corpora after verifying their files by SHA-256, and \texttt{REPRODUCE.md} in the repository walks through the procedure. The RIC loop needs a Linux host or WSL2, and the repository builds FlexRIC, the xApp, and Zeek in user space without administrator rights. All three corpora are public. \DA{} is distributed by its authors under CC-BY-4.0~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}. \DB{} is distributed by its authors under CC-BY-4.0~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd}, including the release with all fields preserved and the GTP-removed captures we used for the address audit and the Zeek re-extraction. \DC{} is available from its authors' repository~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}, which states no license, so we do not redistribute it. We judge it smaller than the benefit to operators of knowing that reported accuracy need not carry over to their networks.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Flow exporters.} Three different tools produced \DA{}, \DB{}, and \DC{} (Zeek, Argus, and NFStream). We re-extracted \DB{} with Zeek, and the gap between \DA{} and \DB{} did not close (Section~\ref{sec:res:anatomy}). The Zeek version and configuration behind \DA{} are undocumented, though, so our Zeek export of \DB{} may still differ from it in timeouts or in its handling of single-packet flows. The Zeek flows take their labels from the published ones through the host pairs of each capture. That rule reproduces every published label on the Argus flows, but a Zeek flow whose host pair carries both attack and benign traffic within one capture would receive the attack label. \DC{} was not re-extracted.

\emph{Target labels.} \LcBenignTwinPct\% of the benign flows in \DB{} are exact copies of records labeled UDP flood elsewhere in the corpus (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}). Their addresses mark them as the flood of the attacker at the other base station. The dataset authors have not confirmed this, and until they do, every operating point on \DB{} appears with and without these flows. The capture files themselves were recovered from the restarts of \texttt{Offset}. The per-station files of the release confirm how the two passes map onto the base stations, since each of the \LcSiteFiles{} files of the first station matches exactly one capture file of the first pass in row count and attacking host (\LcSiteMatched{} of \LcSiteFiles).

\emph{Radio capture schedule.} Nine of the ten benign radio sessions were recorded six weeks before the first attack session, mostly with a single UE, and every radio label is a session label. A radio detector may thus learn to tell capture periods apart as well as attacks. The one benign session recorded during the attack period is flagged almost entirely. So are two early sessions (Table~\ref{tab:benign}), however, and ten benign sessions are too few to decide between the two explanations.

\emph{Number of corpora.} Three corpora are enough to show that the transfer gap recurs, not to describe how it varies across deployments. The third comes from a 5G core testbed rather than an O-RAN deployment, contains only flood attacks and one control-plane attack, and consists of three capture files, too few for cluster-based intervals. We found no other O-RAN corpus with paired radio telemetry.

\emph{Latency.} Every latency figure comes from one workstation without CPU isolation. The RIC loop ran FlexRIC's software path over loopback SCTP to an emulated E2 node with synthetic KPM values. There was no radio, no real E2 node, and no network between sites. What the loop measures is the processing cost of the RIC path, not the delay of a deployment. Because it ran faster at a 1\,ms than at a 10\,ms reporting period, its figures evidently also depend on the host's power management. CPU and memory utilization under load were not measured.

\emph{Operational parameters and the deployability test.} The prevalence $\pi = 0.002$ and the benign arrival rate $\lambda_b$ are declared, not measured on an operational network. We vary both, and operational precision depends on $\pi$ alone. The bounds of the deployability test are our own choice (Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate}). Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} shows the verdict under other bounds, but an operator may weigh recall, precision, and alert volume differently, and the test ignores detection delay and the cost of a missed attack.

\emph{Sample size.} The radio layer has 30 capture sessions with one scenario each, so session and attack scenario are confounded, and the protocol and temporal analyses can detect only large effects. With just ten benign sessions, the radio false positive rate, and with it the operational precision, carries a wide interval (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}). The transfer statistics vary the training set over 20 seeds but always test on one fixed target corpus. They do not capture the variability across other deployments.

\emph{Scope.} We study binary detection only. Multi-class attribution, feature attribution~\cite{lundberg2017shap}, and the fusion of radio and flow features remain open. Because the published summary files cannot be joined record by record, fusion needs the raw archives, as in~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}. Three attack categories of \DA{} are missing from \DB{} and can be tested in one direction only.

\section{Conclusion and Reporting Requirements}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A held-out score on one corpus says little about how a detection xApp will behave in another deployment. On the corpora we examined, a random split inflates the score, and a model-free lookup gains about as much, which fits the recognition of capture sessions. The radio corpus's capture schedule rules out any estimate of temporal drift, and its false positive rate depends largely on which benign session a detector meets. The published 99.9\% on the target corpus depends on record-position fields. Half of that corpus lies on records that occur under both labels, and the addresses of the benign copies identify them as flood traffic from a second attacker. On flows with consistent labels, transferred detectors reach a balanced accuracy of \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}, below what the target corpus itself allows. On a third corpus they catch the SYN flood but flag much of the benign traffic. At a realistic base rate no threshold gives usable operational precision. Only latency passes. Through a FlexRIC loop with an emulated E2 node, a decision and its control message take at most \RicLoopTenPnnHiMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile, most of it spent outside inference. We therefore propose that any study claiming a deployable O-RAN detection capability report at least the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item performance on a corpus collected independently of the training data, in a prevalence-invariant metric such as balanced accuracy or ROC-AUC, beside input-blind baselines and a reference trained inside that corpus;
\item the split protocol and its group key, results under a group-disjoint split, and the category coverage of any time-based split;
\item the false positive rate on benign traffic with its unit, the resulting alert rate, and the operational precision at a stated base rate, all from pooled confusion counts with intervals that resample clusters;
\item the operating threshold, how it was chosen, and the recall it gives up;
\item intervals that account for overlap between resampled splits;
\item tail latency of the loop from indication to control acknowledgment, with its stages, implementation, reporting period, and host, measured through a RIC and, before any conformance claim, with a real E2 node;
\item evidence that the labels are a function of the features used, with no identical records under opposite labels, and that no record-position or identifier field is a feature.
\end{enumerate}
