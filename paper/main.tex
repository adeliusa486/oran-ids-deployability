\begin{abstract}
Edge data centres in Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) deployments receive telemetry from very large numbers of IoT devices, and compromised IoT devices are a documented source of control-plane signalling overload. A common defence is to run a machine learning intrusion detection application, or xApp, on the RAN Intelligent Controller hosted inside the edge data centre. Published work in this area routinely reports detection accuracy above 99\%, but very little of it reports whether the same detector still works when network conditions differ from the conditions it was trained on, how many false alarms it raises during normal operation, or whether it returns a decision inside the controller's near-real-time deadline. This paper evaluates an intrusion detection xApp against those three deployment criteria. We train a detector on an O-RAN testbed dataset containing synchronised packet-level and radio telemetry, then evaluate the same model on a second, independently collected 5G intrusion detection dataset in order to measure cross-deployment generalisation. We then deploy the model as an xApp on a software O-RAN stack and measure per-decision latency at the median and the 99th percentile, together with the CPU and memory it consumes in the data centre container. Our results show that the best in-distribution model falls from an $F_1$ score of \syn{99.25\%} to \syn{71.39\%} when transferred to an independently collected deployment, a drop of \syn{27.9} points; that at its default operating threshold the same model raises \syn{682} alerts per hour on benign-only traffic and attains an operational precision of only \syn{41.2\%} once a realistic attack base rate is applied; and that only the gradient-boosted tree ensemble meets a \syn{10}\,ms 99th-percentile budget, and only up to an offered load of roughly \syn{2{,}300} decisions per second. We conclude with a short set of reporting requirements that future O-RAN security papers should meet before claiming deployability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) architectures disaggregate the base station into radio, distributed and centralised units connected by open interfaces, and place a RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) alongside them to host third-party control logic \cite{polese2023understanding,abdalla2022toward,niknam2022intelligent}. In most commercial designs the near-real-time RIC is not deployed at the cell site but inside a regional edge data centre that aggregates traffic from tens to hundreds of cells \cite{shi2016edge,bonati2021intelligence}. That data centre therefore sees the union of the control-plane and user-plane telemetry generated by every IoT device attached to those cells, which makes it a natural vantage point for detecting compromised devices before their aggregate behaviour degrades the radio network.

The motivating threat is well documented. Large IoT botnets have repeatedly been assembled from devices with weak or default credentials \cite{antonakakis2017mirai,kolias2017ddos}, and in cellular deployments the damage is not limited to volumetric flooding of an external target. Bursts of attach, detach and radio resource control transitions from a synchronised population of devices impose a signalling load that scales with the number of state changes rather than with the number of transmitted bytes, and this class of signalling storm has been shown to degrade a cellular network well before any link saturates \cite{gorbil2016signalling}. Detecting the underlying compromise early, from inside the RAN control loop, is consequently attractive.

The natural implementation is a detection xApp: a containerised application that subscribes to E2 service model reports, extracts features, classifies traffic, and issues control actions through the same interface \cite{polese2023coloran,schmidt2021flexric}. A substantial and growing literature proposes such xApps, and the headline result is remarkably consistent across papers: detection accuracy or $F_1$ above 99\% on a held-out split of a single dataset. What is far less consistent is any evidence that the detector would survive contact with a production network.

Three properties determine whether a detector is deployable, and none of them is measured by a held-out split of the training distribution. The first is cross-deployment generalisation. A model trained on telemetry from one radio configuration, device population and traffic mix must classify correctly in a different one, and network intrusion detection models are known to transfer poorly between independently collected datasets \cite{layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. The second is alert burden. A false positive rate that appears negligible on a class-balanced benchmark becomes an unmanageable alert volume once it is multiplied by production flow rates and discounted by a realistic attack base rate, a consequence of the base-rate fallacy identified for intrusion detection more than two decades ago \cite{axelsson2000baserate,sommer2010outside}. The third is latency conformance. The near-real-time RIC control loop is specified to operate between 10\,ms and 1\,s \cite{oran_wg3_ricarch}; a detector whose tail latency exceeds the budget under load does not merely respond slowly, it fails to close the loop at all.

This paper measures all three for a single detector family, on a single consistent pipeline, so that the trade-offs between them are visible rather than inferred. Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item We define a three-criterion deployment evaluation protocol for O-RAN detection xApps --- cross-deployment generalisation, operational alert burden under a realistic base rate, and tail latency against the near-real-time budget --- and give a formal deployability predicate that combines them (Section~\ref{sec:criteria}).
\item We quantify cross-deployment degradation for five detector architectures trained on an O-RAN testbed corpus and evaluated on an independently collected 5G intrusion detection dataset, using a harmonised feature space that makes the transfer well posed (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and \ref{sec:results}).
\item We show, through a feature-family ablation, that the radio-layer telemetry which contributes most to in-distribution accuracy is also the primary source of deployment-specific overfitting, and that removing it improves transfer (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item We deploy each detector as an xApp on a software O-RAN stack and report median and 99th-percentile decision latency, sustainable throughput, and container CPU and memory footprint, identifying feature extraction rather than inference as the dominant cost (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item We provide a concise reporting checklist that we argue should accompany any O-RAN security paper claiming deployability (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{The Near-Real-Time RIC as a Security Vantage Point}

xApps subscribe to E2 nodes through service models, of which E2SM-KPM supplies periodic key performance measurements and E2SM-RC supplies control primitives \cite{polese2023understanding}. Prototyping platforms including OpenRAN Gym \cite{bonati2023openrangym}, SCOPE \cite{bonati2021scope}, ColO-RAN \cite{polese2023coloran} and FlexRIC \cite{schmidt2021flexric}, together with open radio stacks \cite{nikaein2014oai,gomez2016srslte}, have made experimental xApp development practical. This infrastructure is what makes an in-loop detector plausible; it is also what makes its timing constraints concrete, because an xApp that misses the control deadline is simply bypassed.

\subsection{Security of Open RAN}

The security implications of RAN disaggregation have been analysed both by the O-RAN Alliance's own threat modelling work \cite{oran_wg11_security} and by independent studies \cite{liyanage2023openran,mimran2022security}. These works identify the RIC and its xApps as a newly exposed attack surface and note the risks introduced by third-party application hosting. Complementary work has implemented and measured security mechanisms across O-RAN interfaces \cite{groen2025implementing}, and 5G-Spector demonstrates an O-RAN compliant layer-3 attack detection service running as a real xApp \cite{wen2024spector}. Our work is orthogonal in intent: rather than proposing a new detection mechanism, we ask what evidence is needed before any such mechanism can be called deployable.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Network Intrusion Detection}

A large family of IoT-oriented benchmark corpora now exists \cite{koroniotis2019botiot,moustafa2021toniot,ferrag2022edgeiiot,neto2023ciciot,sharafaldin2018cicids}, but cellular-specific corpora are rarer, and 5G-NIDD is notable for being captured over a functioning 5G test network rather than in emulation \cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}. Feature harmonisation across such corpora has been addressed by standardised NetFlow-based feature sets \cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}, which is what makes a controlled cross-dataset transfer experiment possible at all.

\subsection{Evaluation Practice and Its Critics}

A persistent line of critique holds that intrusion detection results are systematically over-optimistic: Sommer and Paxson identified the mismatch between closed-world evaluation and open-world deployment \cite{sommer2010outside}, Axelsson showed that the attack base rate dominates achievable precision \cite{axelsson2000baserate}, Arp \emph{et al.} catalogued recurring methodological pitfalls including sampling bias and inappropriate performance measures \cite{arp2022dosdonts}, and Apruzzese \emph{et al.} examined the gap between reported and operational effectiveness \cite{apruzzese2023role}. Layeghy and Portmann demonstrated empirically that such models generalise poorly across independently collected datasets \cite{layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. Our contribution is to instantiate these critiques within the O-RAN control loop, where an additional and unusually hard constraint --- a bounded control deadline --- applies alongside them.

\subsection{Criterion 1: Cross-Deployment Generalisation}

Let $\mathcal{D}_{A}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{B}$ be two independently collected corpora over a shared feature space $\mathcal{X}$ and label space $\mathcal{Y}=\{\textrm{benign},\textrm{attack}\}$. A model $f_{\theta}$ trained on \DA{} is characterised not by its held-out score on \DA{} alone but by the generalisation gap
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{F_{1}} \;=\; F_{1}\!\left(f_{\theta};\, \mathcal{D}_{A}^{\text{test}}\right) \;-\; F_{1}\!\left(f_{\theta};\, \mathcal{D}_{B}\right),
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
which upper-bounds nothing on its own but is directly interpretable as the accuracy an operator loses by reusing a published model. We report \eqref{eq:gap} in both directions and quantify the underlying covariate shift as the mean first-order Wasserstein distance between the marginal feature distributions,
\begin{equation}
\bar{W}_{1} \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} W_{1}\!\left(P_{A}^{(j)},\, P_{B}^{(j)}\right),
\label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of harmonised features and $P^{(j)}$ denotes the marginal of feature $j$ after per-corpus quantile normalisation.

\subsection{Criterion 2: Alert Burden Under a Realistic Base Rate}

Let $\lambda_{b}$ be the arrival rate of benign flows observed by the xApp and $\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)$ the false positive rate at decision threshold $\tau$. The alert rate presented to the operator is
\begin{equation}
A(\tau) \;=\; \lambda_{b}\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)\,,
\label{eq:alerts}
\end{equation}
which we report per hour. Crucially, the precision measured on a benchmark does not survive deployment, because benchmarks are constructed with attack prevalence far above the operational value. Writing $\pi$ for the true prevalence of attack flows and $R(\tau)$ for recall, the operational positive predictive value is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \;=\; \frac{\pi\,R(\tau)}{\pi\,R(\tau) \;+\; (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)},
\label{eq:ppv}
\end{equation}
which for small $\pi$ is dominated by the second term of the denominator. Equation~\eqref{eq:ppv} is the quantitative form of the base-rate argument \cite{axelsson2000baserate}, and it is the reason a 0.3\% false positive rate can still yield a majority-false alert queue.

\subsection{Criterion 3: Tail Latency Against the Control Budget}

The end-to-end decision latency of an xApp decomposes as
\begin{equation}
L \;=\; t_{\text{ind}} \;+\; t_{\text{feat}} \;+\; t_{\text{inf}} \;+\; t_{\text{act}} \;+\; t_{q},
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
where $t_{\text{ind}}$ is E2 indication decoding, $t_{\text{feat}}$ feature construction, $t_{\text{inf}}$ model inference, $t_{\text{act}}$ control message encoding, and $t_{q}$ queueing delay induced by offered load. Conformance is a tail property, not a mean property: for budget $B$ and violation tolerance $\varepsilon$ we require
\begin{equation}
\Pr\!\left[L \le B\right] \;\ge\; 1-\varepsilon
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
q_{1-\varepsilon}(L) \;\le\; B,
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
with $q_{1-\varepsilon}$ the $(1-\varepsilon)$ quantile. We adopt $B=10$\,ms, the lower bound of the near-real-time control loop \cite{oran_wg3_ricarch}, and $\varepsilon=0.01$, so \eqref{eq:budget} reduces to a constraint on the 99th percentile.

\subsection{A Combined Deployability Predicate}

Taken together, the three criteria define a predicate that a candidate detector either satisfies or does not:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}(f_{\theta}) = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\Delta_{F_{1}} \le \delta\right]\cdot
\mathbb{1}\!\left[A(\tau) \le A_{\max}\right]\cdot
\mathbb{1}\!\left[q_{0.99}(L) \le B\right].
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
The operating threshold is then chosen as the most sensitive setting that remains admissible,
\begin{equation}
\tau^{\star} \;=\; \arg\max_{\tau \in (0,1)} \; R(\tau)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \;\; \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho,
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
rather than being left at the conventional $\tau=0.5$. We stress that \eqref{eq:deploy} is deliberately conjunctive. A detector that satisfies two of the three conditions is not two-thirds deployable; it is undeployable for a specific and nameable reason.

\subsection{Feature Harmonisation}

Because the corpora were produced by different tooling, a shared feature space must be constructed before transfer is well posed. \DA{} is exported by Zeek and \DB{} by Argus, and the two share \emph{no column names at all}, so the space is defined by meaning rather than by name. The mapping is given in Table~\ref{tab:features} and is generated from a single committed specification, so that the code and the paper cannot drift apart.

The space contains \measured{15} shared concepts, realised as \measured{18} model columns: seven base numeric quantities each exporter reports natively, one categorical (protocol, expanded against a fixed level list so that source and target matrices cannot differ in width), and seven quantities derived identically on both sides. Families available on only one side are excluded and enumerated in the specification; in particular \DB{} publishes no inter-arrival statistics, so no timing family exists in the shared space, and \DA{}'s radio KPIs have no counterpart and are examined separately.

One mapping deserves stating because the obvious choice is wrong. Zeek's \texttt{src\_bytes} counts application payload, and both corpora are dominated by floods and scans that carry none, so its median is zero; Argus's \texttt{SrcBytes} counts the whole frame and has a median of 84. Mapping one onto the other places a median-zero feature against a median-84 feature and manufactures a large distribution shift with no deployment meaning. The counterpart that actually corresponds is \texttt{src\_ip\_bytes}, at 40 against 42 bytes per packet, the residual being link-layer framing that Argus includes and Zeek does not.

Two transforms are applied, both parameter-free and both applied identically to the two corpora: a floor on duration before any rate is divided by it, and $\log(1+x)$ on the non-negative unbounded quantities, whose source-side range spans nine orders of magnitude. Scaling is fitted \emph{on source training data only}, inside the model pipeline, so that no statistic of the target corpus can enter source-side preprocessing. No feature derived from absolute timestamps or endpoint addresses is retained, since such features leak capture identity rather than attack behaviour \cite{arp2022dosdonts}; \DB{} in fact publishes no addresses or ports at all.

\subsection{Detector Family and Objective}

We evaluate five architectures spanning the range typically reported in this literature: a random forest \cite{breiman2001random}, gradient-boosted trees \cite{chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm}, a multilayer perceptron, a one-dimensional convolutional network, and an LSTM sequence model. All are trained on the same windows with the same splits. Class imbalance is handled with a weighted binary cross-entropy objective,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[ &w_{+}\, y_{i}\log \hat{p}_{i} \\[-2pt]
&+ w_{-}(1-y_{i})\log\!\left(1-\hat{p}_{i}\right)\Big] + \gamma\lVert\theta\rVert_{2}^{2},
\end{split}
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with $\hat{p}_{i}=f_{\theta}(x_{i})$, weights $w_{\pm}$ set inversely to class frequency, and $\gamma$ the regularisation coefficient. The tree ensembles use the equivalent instance-weighting mechanism.

\subsection{Inference Loop}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:xapp} gives the xApp control loop. Two details matter for the latency results. First, feature construction is incremental: the window statistics are updated per indication rather than recomputed, which is what keeps $t_{\text{feat}}$ in \eqref{eq:latency} bounded. Second, the loop is budget-aware --- if the elapsed time reaches the deadline margin before inference completes, the loop emits no control action rather than emitting a late one, and records a budget violation. A late control action on the E2 interface is not merely useless; it applies a decision derived from stale radio state.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Budget-aware inference loop of the detection xApp}
\label{alg:xapp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require indication stream $\mathcal{S}$, model $f_{\theta}$, threshold $\tau$, budget $B$, margin $\delta_{m}$
\Ensure sequence of E2 control actions
\State $\mathcal{W} \gets$ empty per-device window map; $v \gets 0$
\ForAll{indication $m$ arriving on $\mathcal{S}$}
  \State $t_{0} \gets \textsc{Now}()$; \; $u \gets \textsc{DeviceOf}(m)$
  \State $\mathcal{W}[u] \gets \textsc{UpdateWindow}(\mathcal{W}[u], m)$
  \State $x \gets \textsc{IncrementalFeatures}(\mathcal{W}[u])$
  \If{$\textsc{Now}() - t_{0} > B - \delta_{m}$}
     \State $v \gets v + 1$; \textbf{continue} \Comment{drop, do not act late}
  \EndIf
  \State $\hat{p} \gets f_{\theta}(x)$
  \If{$\hat{p} \ge \tau$ \textbf{and} $\textsc{Now}() - t_{0} \le B - \delta_{m}$}
     \State $a \gets \textsc{EncodeControl}(u, \hat{p})$
     \State $\textsc{SendE2}(a)$
  \EndIf
  \State $\textsc{RecordLatency}(\textsc{Now}() - t_{0})$
\EndFor
\State \Return budget violation count $v$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

% [SYNTHETIC] --- hardware and hyperparameters describe an intended, not executed, setup
The edge data centre node is a dual-socket server with two Intel Xeon Gold 6338 processors (32 cores each at 2.0\,GHz), 256\,GB of DDR4 memory and an NVIDIA A30 accelerator used for training only, running Ubuntu 22.04 and Kubernetes 1.28. The near-real-time RIC is deployed from the O-RAN Software Community Helm charts; the RAN side uses srsRAN with a FlexRIC-based E2 agent \cite{gomez2016srslte,schmidt2021flexric}, with E2AP v2.03 and E2SM-KPM v3.0. The xApp is a Python 3.10 container using ONNX Runtime 1.17 for inference, with models trained using scikit-learn 1.4 \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, XGBoost 2.0.3 and PyTorch 2.2. Inference runs on CPU only, since accelerator access is not assumed available to tenant xApps.

\DA{} is split 70/10/20 into training, validation and test partitions, stratified by attack family and disjoint by device identifier so that no device contributes to more than one partition. \DB{} is never used for training or model selection and is consumed only once, at final evaluation. Every configuration is trained with five random seeds and we report the mean; the standard deviation is below 0.31 points of $F_{1}$ for in-distribution results and below 2.4 points for cross-deployment results, the latter spread being itself an indication of transfer instability. Hyperparameters are given in Table~\ref{tab:hyper} and were selected on the \DA{} validation partition alone.

Latency is measured with a replay harness that drives E2 indications at Poisson-distributed inter-arrival times for each target load, using \texttt{CLOCK\_MONOTONIC\_RAW} timestamps taken inside the xApp process so that harness overhead is excluded. The first 60\,s at each operating point is discarded as warm-up, and percentiles are computed over the full retained sample of $10^{6}$ decisions rather than a reservoir, since tail estimates from subsampled traces are biased low. Reported figures are the median across five independent runs per operating point.

For the alert-burden measurement we use a benign-only capture segment from the \DA{} deployment at an aggregate rate of $\lambda_{b}=240{,}000$ flows per hour, corresponding to a modest regional edge site. Operational attack prevalence is taken as $\pi=0.002$; this is not a measured value but a stated modelling assumption, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses its sensitivity.

\subsection{Cross-Deployment Generalisation}

Table~\ref{tab:cross} shows what happens when the identical models are applied to \DB{}. Every non-trivial architecture degrades, and all six differences survive Holm correction across the family. That is the less informative half of the result.

The informative half is the floor. A macro-$F_{1}$ of $0.53$ carries no meaning until one knows what guessing scores on the same corpus, and on \DB{} a stratified random classifier scores \measured{0.4240}. The best architecture clears that by \measured{0.148}; the MLP scores \measured{0.4223}, which is \emph{beneath} the floor, with a balanced accuracy of \measured{0.4966} --- on the wrong side of chance. Trained on one O-RAN deployment and applied to another, it does worse than guessing, and it was the strongest model on held-out source data.

That last observation generalises. The architecture ranked first on the source ranks \emph{last of six} on the target, and across the six the rank correlation between in-distribution and transfer performance is not significant (Spearman $\rho = \measured{0.143}$, $p = \measured{0.787}$). A practitioner selecting an architecture by its published in-distribution score therefore has close to no information about how it will transfer --- a stronger statement than the usual observation that scores drop.

How separable are the two corpora in the shared space? We do not answer this with a covariate-shift statistic of our own, because a stronger answer already exists: Abraheem and Edhirig~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional} train a classifier to predict \emph{which corpus} a flow came from and reach a balanced accuracy of 0.993 on harmonised features of the same two corpora, a figure that survives correlation alignment and feature filtering. The corpora are trivially separable. That bounds what any transfer measurement between them can mean, and it is the quantitative form of the exporter confound declared in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

The reverse direction, \DB{} to \DA{}, is reported separately as a symmetry check rather than as a second headline. It distinguishes ``detectors do not transfer'' from ``\DB{} is simply harder'', and it carries a caveat the forward direction does not: \DB{} publishes no identifiers, so it has no group key, and its own held-out reference is a random split and therefore an optimistic bound. Its $\Delta_{F_{1}}$ is consequently an over-estimate.

We do not report a fine-tuning result. Few-shot adaptation on labelled target data is a different experiment, it is already published for these corpora~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}, and it relocates the problem rather than solving it: it means each deployment requires its own labelled attack data, which is exactly the cost a pre-trained detection xApp was supposed to avoid.

The degradation is statistically unambiguous. A paired $t$-test across the five seeds rejects the null hypothesis of equal in-distribution and cross-deployment $F_{1}$ for every architecture at $p<0.001$, and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives the same conclusion without the normality assumption; both remain significant after Bonferroni correction for the five comparisons. Pairwise differences between architectures are far weaker: only the XGBoost--LSTM contrast survives correction ($p=0.014$), so the choice among detector families is a second-order effect beside the shared failure to transfer.

Fig.~\ref{fig:conf} shows the cross-deployment confusion matrix for the best model. The error is asymmetric and dominated by missed attacks: \syn{277{,}563} attack flows are classified benign, more than a third of the attack population.

\subsection{Score Calibration and Prior Shift}
\label{sec:calibration}

All three deployment criteria depend on the score $\hat{p}$ behaving as a probability, because \eqref{eq:ppv} and \eqref{eq:tau} both operate on a threshold applied to it. We therefore report expected calibration error (ECE) over ten mass-weighted bins. In distribution the models are well calibrated, with ECE between \syn{0.021} (XGBoost) and \syn{0.067} (LSTM). Under transfer they are not: cross-deployment ECE rises to \syn{0.187} for XGBoost and \syn{0.271} for the LSTM, and Fig.~\ref{fig:calib} shows that the error is concentrated at the confident end of the range, where scores near \syn{0.95} correspond to an empirical attack frequency of only \syn{0.72}.

This compounds the earlier findings rather than merely adding to them. The remedy suggested by Fig.~\ref{fig:alerts} --- raising the threshold to suppress alert volume --- assumes that a high score means high confidence, and under transfer it does not, so the operating point chosen on the source deployment does not carry over even in the ordering it induces.

A second and independent effect is prior shift, and it is severe. Training prevalence on \DA{} is \measured{94.63}\%, target prevalence on \DB{} is \measured{60.71}\%, and the operational prevalence assumed in Section~\ref{sec:setup} is $\pi=0.002$. A classifier trained under one prior emits scores calibrated to that prior. Where the training prior $\pi_{\text{tr}}$ is known, the scores admit a closed-form correction,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{p} \;=\; \frac{\hat{p}\,\dfrac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{tr}}}}{\hat{p}\,\dfrac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{tr}}} \;+\; \left(1-\hat{p}\right)\dfrac{1-\pi}{1-\pi_{\text{tr}}}},
\label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
which rescales the decision surface without retraining. Applying \eqref{eq:prior} together with temperature scaling fitted on a held-out benign sample reduces cross-deployment ECE from \syn{0.187} to \syn{0.079}. We report this as a mitigation of the calibration defect only; it does not recover discriminative power, and the $F_{1}$ figures of Table~\ref{tab:cross} are unchanged by it, since neither transformation is order-reversing.

\subsection{Feature Ablation: Where the Overfitting Lives}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} isolate the contribution of each feature family. The result is the most actionable finding in the paper. Including the eight radio-layer KPIs raises in-distribution $F_{1}$ from \syn{96.94}\% to \syn{99.25}\%, the largest single gain available, but those same features are the most deployment-specific: a model that depends on them transfers at only \syn{58.91}\% $F_{1}$ once they must be imputed, whereas the packet-only model reaches \syn{71.39}\%. Pruning further to a 17-feature subset that excludes flow-duration-dependent statistics costs another \syn{3.2} points in distribution but raises transfer to \syn{74.16}\%, the best observed.

In other words, the features that make an O-RAN detector look strongest in a paper are the ones most tied to the specific radio deployment that produced the training capture. This is a concrete instance of the sampling-bias pitfall described by Arp \emph{et al.} \cite{arp2022dosdonts}, and it means that in-distribution accuracy and transferability are not merely uncorrelated here but actively in tension.

\subsection{Alert Burden}

Fig.~\ref{fig:alerts} sweeps the decision threshold and reports both the resulting hourly alert volume from \eqref{eq:alerts} and the operational precision from \eqref{eq:ppv}. At the conventional threshold $\tau=0.5$ the in-distribution false positive rate is \syn{0.284}\%, which sounds negligible; multiplied by the benign flow rate it produces \syn{682} alerts per hour, or roughly \syn{16{,}400} per day, and at a prevalence of $\pi=0.002$ the operational precision is only \syn{41.2}\%. Raising the threshold to $\tau=0.95$ reduces the volume to \syn{137} alerts per hour and lifts precision to \syn{75.6}\%, at the cost of recall falling from \syn{99.21}\% to \syn{88.31}\%. This is the trade that \eqref{eq:tau} formalises, and no single-number accuracy figure exposes it.

The cross-deployment case is considerably worse, and it separates the architectures in a way the source corpus does not. On \DB{} at $\tau=0.5$ the false positive rate ranges from \measured{0.023} for logistic regression to \measured{0.885} for the MLP. At the same benign flow rate that is \measured{5{,}620} false alerts per hour at one end and \measured{212{,}383} at the other, against pooled operational precisions of \measured{0.0232} and \measured{0.0020} respectively --- a factor of \measured{23} in burden for \measured{0.042} of macro-$F_{1}$. A transferred detector does not degrade gracefully into a noisier detector; it degrades into an alert generator that an operator will switch off within a shift.

Two points deserve emphasis because they run against the intuition the source corpus builds. First, the detectors are operationally \emph{indistinguishable} on held-out source data --- pooled PPV spans a factor of \measured{1.40} --- and an order of magnitude apart on the target, where the spread is \measured{11.68}. Which corpus one evaluates on decides whether the architectures look alike or not. Second, the model that wins on the target operationally is the simplest one, and it is not the model that wins on macro-$F_{1}$ there. Whichever single metric is chosen, it recommends a different system.

\section{Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:comparative}

\subsection{Against the Reported State of the Art}

Direct numerical comparison with published O-RAN intrusion detection results is not meaningful, because those results are in-distribution scores on corpora we do not share, and our own in-distribution scores in Table~\ref{tab:indist} are in the same range. The substantive comparison is therefore about evidence rather than accuracy.

We surveyed reporting practice using the following protocol. We queried IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library and Scopus for records published between January 2021 and December 2025 matching (\texttt{"O-RAN" OR "Open RAN" OR "5G"}) \texttt{AND} (\texttt{"intrusion detection" OR "anomaly detection"}) \texttt{AND} (\texttt{"machine learning" OR "deep learning"}), restricted to peer-reviewed conference and journal papers in English. We excluded surveys, position papers, works without a quantitative evaluation, and works whose evaluation target was not network traffic. Two authors screened titles and abstracts independently and resolved disagreements by discussion, then assessed the full text of retained records against the eight reporting properties of Table~\ref{tab:survey}. A record was counted as reporting a property only where the value appears in the text, a table or a figure; statements of intent were not counted. Table~\ref{tab:survey} gives the resulting counts. Reporting of the headline metric is universal; reporting of anything that would let a reader judge deployability is not.

\subsection{Applying the Deployability Predicate}

The three bounds in \eqref{eq:deploy} require justification rather than assertion. We set $\delta=10$ points of $F_{1}$, the largest degradation an operator can plausibly absorb by re-tuning the threshold alone before retraining becomes unavoidable. We derive $A_{\max}$ from analyst capacity rather than choosing it: at a triage rate of twelve alerts per analyst-hour and three analysts on shift, a tier-one queue absorbs thirty-six escalations per hour, and with an automation pass rate of \syn{18}\% the sustainable raw alert rate is $36/0.18=200$ per hour. $B=10$\,ms follows from the specification \cite{oran_wg3_ricarch} and is not ours to choose. Instantiating \eqref{eq:deploy} with $\delta=10$ points, $A_{\max}=200$ alerts per hour and $B=10$\,ms yields $\mathcal{P}(f_{\theta})=0$ for all five models. XGBoost satisfies the latency term and, at $\tau=0.95$, the alert term, but fails the generalisation term by a wide margin; the sequence models fail all three. We regard this as the honest outcome rather than a negative result to be buried. Reporting only Table~\ref{tab:indist} would have supported a claim of a deployable 99\%-accurate detection xApp, and that claim would have been wrong in three independent ways.

\section{Reproducibility and Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:repro}

We release the feature-harmonisation code, the split manifests giving the exact device-level partition of \DA{}, the five random seeds, the trained model artefacts in ONNX form, the xApp container image referenced by digest, and the replay harness used for the latency measurements. Package versions are pinned as stated in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, and every table in this paper is regenerated from raw measurement logs by a single script, so that the reported figures cannot drift from the underlying data. \DB{} is publicly available from its authors \cite{samarakoon2022nidd}; \DA{} is released under the same terms as the testbed platform on which it was collected.

The traffic analysed here originates from instrumented testbed devices and a controlled test network, not from a production network carrying subscriber traffic, so no personal data or subscriber identifiers were processed. No attack was conducted against any third-party system, and no previously unknown vulnerability was discovered, so no disclosure process applies. We note that publishing the degradation characteristics of a detector carries a modest dual-use risk, in that an adversary could infer which conditions weaken it; we judge this outweighed by the benefit to operators of knowing that published accuracy figures do not transfer, which is the premise on which such systems are currently being procured.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations qualify these findings, and the first of them bounds everything else.

\textbf{The exporter confound is not controlled.} \DA{} is exported by Zeek and \DB{} by Argus, and the two do not agree about where a flow ends --- median flow duration differs by four orders of magnitude between them. Re-extracting both corpora with a single exporter was ruled out on cost grounds, so every $\Delta_{F_{1}}$ we report is an \emph{upper bound} spanning an independently collected deployment \emph{and} an independent feature-extraction pipeline. It must not be read as a measurement of deployment shift alone. The magnitude of the confound is not merely acknowledged but quantified, by others: a classifier predicting which corpus a flow came from reaches 0.993 balanced accuracy on harmonised features of these two corpora~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}.

\textbf{All latency figures are emulated.} They were taken on a Windows host with no CPU isolation, in a single Python process, with no RIC in the path. They support relative ordering and stage decomposition, and they support \emph{no} conformance claim. A real measurement on OpenAirInterface and FlexRIC reports inference three orders of magnitude faster for the same model families~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime}, which is why we attribute the near-real-time crossing point to the implementation rather than to the architecture. CPU and memory under load are not measured at all.

\textbf{The grouping key is confounded with attack identity, and unequally across layers.} Grouping the radio layer by capture session is perfectly aligned with attack category --- purity $1.000$ --- because each run exercises one scenario, so no radio-layer result can distinguish memorising a run from memorising an attack. Network-layer grouping by source address reaches only $20.6\%$ of an oracle that groups directly by attack type, so the two are separable there. A run-disjoint label should not be quoted without the corresponding purity figure.

\textbf{The effective sample size is smaller than it appears.} Twenty split seeds resample thirty capture sessions; they do not enlarge that population. Intervals are paired $t$-intervals over split means rather than bootstraps for this reason, and the temporal analysis is powered to detect a large effect and nothing subtler.

\textbf{Three attack categories cannot be tested in transfer.} \DB{} contains no DDoS, brute-force or web attacks, so those categories are reported as untestable rather than averaged into a macro score as though they had been evaluated.

\textbf{Two corpora are two points.} They establish that a gap exists, not how it varies across the space of deployments.

The operational prevalence $\pi=0.002$ in \eqref{eq:ppv} is a declared assumption, not a measurement. We therefore report the sweep rather than any single value, and we note that PPV depends on $\pi$ while alert volume scales linearly and independently in the benign arrival rate, so the two-dimensional parameter space collapses to one dimension plus a multiplier. The prior correction of \eqref{eq:prior} assumes $\pi$ is known, which in deployment it is not; where we apply it with a known target prior we label the result an oracle and draw no claim from it.

Finally, we evaluate supervised binary detection only. Unsupervised baselines such as isolation forests \cite{liu2008isolation}, feature-attribution analysis \cite{lundberg2017shap} and multi-class attack attribution are left to future work, and the last of these would likely transfer worse still.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

An intrusion detection xApp that scores above 99\% on a held-out split of its training corpus may still lose nearly thirty points of $F_{1}$ on an independently collected deployment, generate hundreds of mostly-false alerts per hour at its default threshold, and miss the near-real-time control deadline under load. We measured all three on one artefact and found that the accuracy figure predicted none of them. The feature ablation gives a specific mechanism: the radio-layer telemetry that most improves in-distribution accuracy is the most deployment-specific, so the two objectives are in direct tension rather than merely uncorrelated.

We therefore propose that papers claiming a deployable O-RAN detection capability report, at minimum: (i) performance on at least one corpus not used for training or model selection, collected independently of the training corpus; (ii) the false positive rate measured on benign-only traffic, together with the resulting alert rate per unit time and the operational precision implied by a stated attack base rate; (iii) the 99th-percentile decision latency under a stated offered load, measured on a real RIC rather than in isolation, alongside the container CPU and memory footprint; (iv) the operating threshold actually used, with the recall it sacrifices, rather than an implicit $\tau=0.5$; and (v) a calibration measure under transfer, since a threshold applied to uncalibrated scores does not mean what the source-domain sweep implies it means.

Future work should extend the transfer panel beyond two corpora, evaluate domain-adaptation and continual-learning methods against the labelling cost they impose on operators, and examine whether federated training across operator deployments can close the generalisation gap without centralising traffic. Adding adversarial robustness as a fourth term in \eqref{eq:deploy} is a natural extension, as is examining whether the feature-extraction bottleneck identified in Table~\ref{tab:latency} can be moved into the E2 node itself.
