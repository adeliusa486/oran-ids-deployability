\begin{abstract}
Several 5G and O-RAN intrusion detection papers report more than 99\% accuracy on a random split of one corpus. Earlier studies measured transfer or latency separately. We measure transfer, alert burden, and latency for six architectures and two input-blind baselines on three public corpora (NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD, 5G-NIDD, and a 5G core testbed). On the radio layer, a random split raises macro-$F_1$ by \LeakAvg{} over a session-disjoint split (95\% interval \LeakAvgNbLo{} to \LeakAvgNbHi{}), and a nearest-neighbor lookup gains \NNGain{}, consistent with recognition of capture sessions. In 5G-NIDD, \LcBenignTwinPct\% of benign flows are copies of flood records labeled as attacks, and their addresses point to the attacker at the other base station. The copies cap the balanced accuracy of any content-only classifier at \LcCeilNative{}. Two record-position fields tell them apart: without these fields the reproduced published pipeline falls from 99.9\% to \PubOneNoposAccMax\% accuracy, yet it stays near 100\% on flows without the copies. On flows with consistent labels, detectors transferred to 5G-NIDD reach \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{} balanced accuracy, below in-target references (\TgtRandShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtFileShCleanBAmax{}), with a loss against held-out source data that is significant for \TrCleanSig{} of six architectures. A common exporter does not close the transfer gap. At a declared prevalence of 0.002, no transferred detector exceeds a precision of \ThAPPVbest{} at recall above 10\%, and none passes our deployability test. Window-level detectors meet its latency term in a FlexRIC loop with an emulated E2 node (99th percentile at most \RicLoopTenPnnMax{}\,ms). A random-split score is not evidence of deployability.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) splits the base station into radio, distributed, and central units joined by open interfaces and adds a RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) that runs third-party control applications~\cite{polese2023understanding,abdalla2022toward,niknam2022intelligent}. The near-real-time RIC sits close to the cells it controls~\cite{bonati2021intelligence,polese2023understanding} and receives the per-device measurements that their E2 nodes report under its subscriptions. A detection application (xApp) in the RIC could therefore look for attacks close to where they enter the network, for example from compromised IoT devices, a well-documented source of volumetric attacks~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai,kolias2017ddos}. Platforms for building xApps exist~\cite{polese2023coloran,schmidt2021flexric}, and detectors in or beside the O-RAN control loop have been built and evaluated, each in its own testbed~\cite{wen2024spector,scalingi2024detran}.

Several studies of machine-learning intrusion detection for 5G evaluate on a random held-out split of one dataset and report accuracy or $F_1$ above 99\%; on 5G-NIDD, one of our three corpora, the dataset authors report binary accuracies of 99.85\% to 99.95\% for four of five classifiers~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv}, and later work a weighted $F_1$ of up to 99.95\%~\cite{ilias2024moe5g}. A random split measures how well a detector separates the records of one corpus, not the three properties that deployment depends on: generalization to a site that contributed no training data, where intrusion detectors are known to transfer poorly~\cite{layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}; alert burden, since a false positive rate that looks small on a balanced benchmark becomes a stream of alerts at production flow rates and a realistic attack base rate~\cite{axelsson2000baserate,sommer2010outside}; and latency, since the O-RAN E2 specification allows near-real-time control loops of 10\,ms to 1\,s~\cite{etsi_e2gap}. We ask what a held-out score on one corpus predicts about these properties, and, unable to observe a deployment, study generalization through transfer to independently collected corpora.

For NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD and 5G-NIDD, two of our corpora, Abraheem and Edhirig measured transfer~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional} and Obiuwevwi et al. timed inference inside a real near-real-time RIC~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime}. We offer neither a new detector nor the finding that transfer hurts, but measure random-split leakage, transfer loss, alert burden, and latency for the same six architectures, with input-blind baselines on every corpus, so that the sizes of these effects can be compared. Doing so exposed a labeling fault: half of 5G-NIDD lies on records that occur under both labels, and a published 99.9\% on it depends on two fields that encode a record's position in its file. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item A ladder of evaluation protocols, from a random split to splits that hold out capture sessions, capture files, base stations, and whole corpora. On the radio layer the random-split gain is consistent with the recognition of capture sessions, and the capture schedule rules out a drift estimate (Sections~\ref{sec:res:protocol} and~\ref{sec:res:drift}).
\item An audit of the 5G-NIDD labels that traces the benign-labeled flood copies to the attacker at the other base station, and the published pipeline moved down the same ladder (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}).
\item Separate tests of what the gap between corpora consists of: prior shift, label conflicts, the flow exporter, and the traffic itself (a domain classifier and a third corpus) (Sections~\ref{sec:res:transfer} to~\ref{sec:res:third}).
\item Operational precision and alert volume from pooled confusion counts with cluster-bootstrap intervals, where a mean of per-split precision would inflate differences between architectures by an order of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}).
\item Which calibration methods can change the reachable operating points; none raises target balanced accuracy here, and label-free estimates of the target prior fail once the class-conditional distributions shift (Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration}).
\item Decision latency on one host for three implementations, and the full loop of a window-level detector running as an xApp in FlexRIC with an emulated E2 node (Section~\ref{sec:res:latency}).
\item A deployability test that combines generalization, alert burden, and latency, its sensitivity to its bounds, and reporting requirements (Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}

Two limits of scope apply throughout. We measured latency on one host, without a RIC and through FlexRIC with an emulated E2 node, with no radio or operator network. The three corpora also come from three flow exporters, so every cross-corpus gap mixes a change of site with a change of tooling; for \DA{} and \DB{} we reduce this confound by re-extracting \DB{} with Zeek, the exporter family of \DA{}, whose configuration for \DA{} is undocumented (Sections~\ref{sec:res:anatomy} and~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{The Near-Real-Time RIC as a Detection Platform}

An xApp talks to E2 nodes through E2 service models (E2SM). E2SM-KPM delivers periodic key performance measurements (KPM), and E2SM-RC exposes control actions~\cite{polese2023understanding}. Building experimental xApps became practical with platforms such as OpenRAN Gym~\cite{bonati2023openrangym}, SCOPE~\cite{bonati2021scope}, ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran}, and FlexRIC~\cite{schmidt2021flexric}, together with open radio stacks~\cite{nikaein2014oai,gomez2016srslte}. E2 carries aggregated measurements, not packets, so a flow-level detector needs a data path of its own (Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice}).

\subsection{Security of Open RAN}

The RIC and its third-party xApps are themselves a new attack surface, according to the O-RAN Alliance threat model~\cite{etsi_wg11_threat} and independent analyses~\cite{liyanage2023openran,mimran2022security}, and security mechanisms for the O-RAN interfaces have since been built and evaluated~\cite{groen2025implementing}. On the detection side, 5G-Spector offers layer-3 attack detection as an O-RAN compliant service~\cite{wen2024spector}, Det-RAN detects attacks from cross-layer data in real time~\cite{scalingi2024detran}, and Fard et al. fuse radio telemetry and flow records on our source corpus~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}. Each is evaluated in the setting it was built for; our question is what such an evaluation predicts about another site.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Network Intrusion Detection}

Public intrusion corpora are plentiful for IoT traffic~\cite{koroniotis2019botiot,moustafa2021toniot,ferrag2022edgeiiot,neto2023ciciot,sharafaldin2018cicids} and scarce for cellular networks. 5G-NIDD was captured on an operational 5G test network~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, and NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD pairs flow records with radio telemetry from an O-RAN testbed~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}. Standardized NetFlow feature sets made cross-corpus experiments easier~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}. The labels and flow records of a corpus can themselves be wrong: Engelen et al. documented labeling and flow-construction faults in CIC-IDS2017~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}, and Flood et al. found design flaws across NIDS benchmarks that let a classifier score well without learning the attack~\cite{flood2024smells}.

\subsection{Evaluation Practice and Dataset Shift}

Reported results tend to overstate what a detector can do, for well-known reasons: closed-world evaluation~\cite{sommer2010outside}, neglect of the base rate~\cite{axelsson2000baserate}, sampling bias and data leakage~\cite{arp2022dosdonts,kapoor2023leakage}, and temporal or spatial bias in the split~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}. Apruzzese et al. trace the gap between reported and operational effectiveness across security domains~\cite{apruzzese2023role}, Kus et al. find it in industrial intrusion detection once detectors meet data they were not tuned on~\cite{kus2022falsesense}, and cross-dataset generalization has repeatedly proved poor~\cite{dhooge2020interdataset,layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}.

Closest to our work, Abraheem and Edhirig transfer XGBoost between the same two corpora with 15 harmonized features and report zero-shot balanced accuracies per attack family of 0.50 to 0.56 from NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD to 5G-NIDD and 0.52 to 0.83 in the other direction~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}. We add input-blind baselines, in-target references, the published pipeline under the same protocol ladder, an audit of the target labels, and the operational and latency criteria.

From the dataset-shift literature we take label-shift estimation from unlabeled data~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,lipton2018bbse}, which assumes that only the class balance changes, calibration and its behavior under shift~\cite{guo2017calibration,ovadia2019trust,fawcett2007pav}, shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing}, and covariance alignment~\cite{sun2016coral}, which matches the second moments of the source features to the target. Classifiers compared under repeated resampling are tested as Nadeau and Bengio recommend~\cite{nadeau2003inference}.

\section{Deployment Criteria and Threat Model}
\label{sec:criteria}

\subsection{System and Threat Model}

A near-real-time RIC serves a set of E2 nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) to which user devices, IoT devices among them, attach. The adversary controls some devices and uses them for flooding, scanning, brute-force login, web attacks, or, in one corpus, an attack on the control plane of the core network (PFCP session deletion), the attack categories in our corpora. It does not control the RIC, the E2 interface, or the xApp. The defender's xApp builds radio telemetry windows from E2SM-KPM reports or reads flow records from a user-plane exporter outside the RIC (Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice}), and it can act through E2SM-RC.

\subsection{Criterion 1: Generalization Across Corpora}

Let \DA{} and \DB{} be two independently collected corpora in a shared feature space. We measure a model $f_{\theta}$ trained on \DA{} by how far its score on \DB{} falls below its held-out score on \DA{}. The corpora differ in attack prevalence (Table~\ref{tab:corpora}), and a change in macro-$F_1$ blends a change in the classifier with a change in class balance: even a classifier that ignores its input scores differently when the prevalence moves. We therefore use the difference in balanced accuracy (BA, the mean of the true positive and true negative rates), which prevalence does not affect,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{BA}} = \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\mathrm{test}}\right) - \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{B}\right),
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
and we also give the macro-$F_1$ gap $\Delta_{F_1}$, defined in the same way (source minus target), and its difference from the gap of the majority-class baseline. As a descriptive diagnostic that is not part of the test, we measure covariate shift one feature at a time,
\begin{equation}
\bar{W}_{1} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} W_{1}\!\left(Q_{j}(P_{A}^{(j)}),\, Q_{j}(P_{B}^{(j)})\right),
\label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of 18 shared columns, $P_{A}^{(j)}$ and $P_{B}^{(j)}$ are the marginal distributions of column $j$ in \DA{} and \DB{}, $W_{1}$ is the first-order Wasserstein distance, and $Q_{j}$ is the quantile transform of column $j$, fitted on source training data only. Had each corpus been normalized by its own quantiles, both marginals would become uniform and every term would vanish.

\subsection{Criterion 2: Alert Burden Under a Realistic Base Rate}

Let $\lambda_{b}$ be the benign arrival rate in the detector's own unit, which is flow records per hour for a flow-level detector and telemetry windows per hour for a window-level one. A benign user equipment (UE) produces 225 non-overlapping windows of 16\,s per hour. For a decision threshold $\tau$ on the detector's score, let $\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)$ be the false positive rate and $R(\tau)$ the recall. False alerts then arrive at
\begin{equation}
A(\tau) = \lambda_{b}\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau),
\label{eq:alerts}
\end{equation}
and, for an operational attack prevalence $\pi$, the fraction of alerts that correspond to attacks is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PPV}(\tau) = \frac{\pi R(\tau)}{\pi R(\tau) + (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)}.
\label{eq:ppv}
\end{equation}
We call this PPV the operational precision; the corpus precision is the precision at the corpus's own attack prevalence. The corpora cannot give us $\pi$ or $\lambda_{b}$, so we declare both (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Only $\pi$ enters the PPV, and $A$ grows linearly with $\lambda_{b}$, so alert counts at another $\lambda_{b}$ follow from the false positive rates we report.

\subsection{Criterion 3: Latency Against the Control Budget}

The decision latency of an xApp can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}
L = t_{\mathrm{ind}} + t_{\mathrm{feat}} + t_{\mathrm{inf}} + t_{\mathrm{act}} + t_{q},
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
where the terms are the delivery of the indication to the xApp, feature construction, inference, the control action up to its acknowledgment, and queueing inside the xApp. We time feature construction and inference alone on one host, and all five terms for window-level detectors in a loop through a near-real-time RIC with an emulated E2 node (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). What matters for conformance is the tail: for a budget $B$ and a tolerated violation rate $\varepsilon$, we require $q_{1-\varepsilon}(L) \le B$. The test of Section~\ref{sec:crit:test} uses the strictest budget the E2 specification allows, $B = 10$\,ms, and we also report the smallest budget $B_{\min}$ each detector meets.

\subsection{A Deployability Test}
\label{sec:crit:test}

The three criteria are combined into a test that a detector either passes or fails:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{P}(f_{\theta}) = {} & \ind\!\left[\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}} \le \delta\right] \cdot \ind\!\left[\overline{q}_{0.99}(L) \le B\right] \\
& \cdot \ind\!\big[\exists\,\tau:\ R(\tau)\ge r_{\min},\ A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \\
& \qquad\quad \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho\big],
\end{split}
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}}$ and $\overline{q}_{0.99}$ are upper 95\% confidence bounds, so a pass cannot hinge on a lucky point estimate. The generalization and alert terms are evaluated on the same target flows; the latency term needs a measured decision loop, which we have for window-level detectors only. The minimum recall $r_{\min}$ keeps a detector that never raises an alert from passing the alert term, and a passing detector would operate at the most sensitive admissible threshold, the one with the highest recall among those meeting all three alert conditions. The test is a reporting instrument, not an O-RAN requirement; its bounds stand for operator requirements, and Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate} gives our values and how the verdict responds when they change.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Corpora}
\label{sec:corpora}

\DA{} is NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}, recorded on a physical OpenAirInterface O-RAN testbed, with benign traffic and five attack categories: denial of service (DoS), distributed denial of service (DDoS), probing, brute force, and web attacks. Its two layers, flow records exported by Zeek at the central unit and radio telemetry sampled at 1\,Hz at the distributed unit, cannot be joined record by record from the published files. For the radio layer we cut the records of each UE into consecutive, non-overlapping windows of 16 records and summarize each window by the mean and standard deviation of 16 KPM fields.

Gaps of more than five minutes in the timestamps split the radio layer into 30 capture sessions, the group key of every radio split. Fard et al. describe the corpus as \SessRunN{} experiment runs~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}, and a gap threshold of \SessRunGapMin{} to \SessRunGapMax{}\,s recovers exactly \SessRunN{} segments, each with a single attack category. Every session is a union of complete segments: the five-minute rule merges runs recorded back to back, keeping them on the same side of any split, and \SessMultiSub{} of the 30 sessions contain two or three attack subcategories, the finer attack labels the corpus gives within a category. All records of a session carry the same category label, so a radio label is a session label. Section~\ref{sec:res:drift} gives the capture schedule.

\DB{} is 5G-NIDD~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, captured at another institution on a 5G test network with two base stations, exported by Argus, and labeled with two attack categories (DoS and probing). Its combined file, \texttt{Combined.csv}, has no addresses, ports, or timestamps but keeps the Argus record counter \texttt{Seq} and the file offset \texttt{Offset}, which restarts at every file boundary, so we can recover 20 capture files, ten per base station (files 1 to 10 from station~1, 11 to 20 from station~2). Both stations went through the same sequence of attack types: nine files each hold benign traffic plus one attack type, and the tenth carries no attack label. A second release, which we call the full-field release, keeps every Argus field, addresses included, and the raw captures are public too.

A third corpus, \DC{}, gives a second transfer target at a third site: the flow records of a 5G core testbed (Open5GS in Docker) published by Nugraha et al.~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}. Three of its files hold flow records, each with one attack (a SYN flood, an ICMP flood, and a PFCP session-deletion attack), for \ThNflows{} flows, \ThNattack{} of them attacks; its benign flows mix user traffic with core-network signaling. NFStream, which exported \DC{}, counts link-layer bytes plus 44 bytes of GTP-U encapsulation per tunneled packet, and we strip both so that the shared columns hold inner IP bytes, as for \DA{}. \subsection{Shared Feature Space}
\label{sec:shared}

Zeek and Argus share no column names, so we define the flow feature space by meaning (Table~\ref{tab:features}): 15 concepts in 18 columns, namely seven base quantities (duration and packet and byte counts), a one-hot protocol with a fixed list of levels, and seven derived quantities computed the same way for every corpus. One mapping is easy to get wrong. Zeek's \texttt{src\_bytes} counts application payload, zero for most floods and scans, whereas Argus's \texttt{SrcBytes} counts whole packets including link-layer framing; its counterpart is Zeek's \texttt{src\_ip\_bytes}, which counts a minimum-size packet as 40 bytes where Argus counts 42. For \DC{}, NFStream's duration, per-direction packet and byte counts (without the overheads above), and protocol number fill the same columns. All corpora get the same 1\,ms floor on duration and $\log(1+x)$ transform for unbounded quantities, scaling is fitted on source training data alone, and no address, port, or timestamp is a feature. Projecting \DA{} onto the shared columns costs \HarmBAmin{} to \HarmBAmax{} of in-distribution balanced accuracy against all \NFullFeat{} non-port Zeek features.

Before computing any result with it, we also fixed a subset of nine columns that we expected to depend less on the exporter, which we call exporter-robust: the protocol and the five per-packet means and direction ratios. Where an exporter ends a flow moves duration, rates, and totals; per-packet means and direction ratios move less but are not invariant, since splitting a flow changes them and the 40- and 42-byte counts differ by about 5\% for minimum-size packets.

\subsection{Detectors}
\label{sec:detectors}

Every protocol is run on six supervised architectures, logistic regression (LR), a decision tree (DT) of depth 12, a random forest (RF)~\cite{breiman2001random}, XGBoost (XGB)~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, histogram gradient boosting (HGB) from scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, and a multilayer perceptron (MLP), and on two input-blind baselines, a majority-class predictor and a stratified sampler of the training prior, whose scores can change between protocols only through class composition. All supervised models use balanced class weights (sample weights for the MLP, \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} for XGB). We fixed the hyperparameters in advance, because tuning on held-out source data would reward source-specific structure, which the transfer experiments measure, and tuning on the target would leak target information into training. LR uses \texttt{max\_iter} 2000 on standardized inputs, RF 200 trees with minimum leaf size 2, XGB 300 trees of depth 6 with $\eta=0.1$, subsample 0.8, and the \texttt{hist} method, HGB 300 iterations of depth 6 with $\eta=0.1$, and the MLP two hidden layers of 128 and 64 units trained with Adam and early stopping (patience 10) on standardized inputs.

Three more model families answer narrower questions. Two sequence models, a gated recurrent unit (GRU) network~\cite{cho2014gru} with 32 hidden units and attack-class weight equal to the benign-to-attack ratio, and a one-dimensional convolutional network (1D-CNN) with two convolutions of 32 filters, kernel 3, and average pooling, read the raw 16-step KPM sequences of the same radio windows. Two unsupervised detectors, an isolation forest (IF)~\cite{liu2008isolation} with 200 trees and an autoencoder (AE) with a 12--6--12 layout scored by reconstruction error, learn from benign source flows only, with the alarm threshold at the 99th percentile of benign source scores. A CORAL variant~\cite{sun2016coral}, the one transfer model that sees target data, re-colors the source flows to the covariance of the unlabeled target before training (per-domain z-scores, identity-regularized covariances).

Two analyses use no detector: a one-nearest-neighbor (1-NN) lookup that gives each radio test window the label of its nearest training window (Euclidean distance on standardized window summaries), and a domain classifier (histogram gradient boosting, 200 iterations) that tells \DA{} from \DB{} flows, scored by balanced accuracy under five-fold stratified cross-validation.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}

Splits are group-disjoint wherever a group key exists, apart from random splits run as a comparison, and an automated check rejects any group-disjoint split with a group on both sides. On the radio layer the group key is the capture session, and three protocols each hold out about 20\% of the windows: a random split, a session-disjoint split, and a category-stratified session-disjoint split that keeps sessions of every attack category on both sides. Two further designs probe time (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}): holding out the latest or earliest session of every category, against control draws that hold out a random session per category, and holding out each benign session alone. On the flow layer the group key is the source address (318 groups). For each of 20 split seeds we draw a seeded sample of 300,000 flows from \DA{} (a size fixed before the first run), hold out about 20\% of it by source address, and evaluate on all of \DB{}. The reverse direction trains on 80\% of a 300,000-flow sample of \DB{} and tests on \DA{}. The in-target references on \DB{} train on 80\% of such a sample and test on the rest, hold out four of the 20 capture files, or train on one base station and test on the other (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}). Apart from these references, CORAL, the domain classifier, the prior estimates of Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration}, and the best-threshold searches reported as upper bounds, no transform, threshold, or model choice touches \DB{}, and every access to it is logged.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

We estimate uncertainty over 20 splits, each defined by its split seed. The splits resample one population of 30 sessions or 318 addresses, so the training sets of different splits overlap, as do their test sets, and a paired $t$-test over split means understates the variance. Our intervals are therefore Nadeau--Bengio corrected~\cite{nadeau2003inference}: the variance is scaled by $1/J + n_{\mathrm{test}}/n_{\mathrm{train}}$ for $J$ splits, with $n$ counting the windows or flows of the source test and training sets. In a transfer comparison the target is fixed, so the interval covers only the variation from resampling the source, not that across target corpora. Holm correction is applied within each family, one metric and one comparison (a protocol pair or a transfer direction) across the six supervised architectures; the two sequence models form a family of their own. Operating points come from confusion counts pooled over splits (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}). A pooled count includes a sample once per split that tests it, and samples in one cluster are correlated, so instead of a binomial interval we use a cluster bootstrap with 2,000 replicates that resamples both splits and clusters (radio sessions, \DA{} source addresses, or \DB{} capture files).

\subsection{Decision Loop of the xApp}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:xapp} gives the decision loop of a detection xApp, which appends each KPM report to the window of its UE and scores the window once it holds 16 records, one decision per UE every 16\,s. The deadline check compares the time $\ell$ spent inside the xApp with an in-xApp budget $B_{x}$: $B$ minus the time the operator reserves for delivering the indication ($t_{\mathrm{ind}}$) and acknowledging the control message (part of $t_{\mathrm{act}}$). A late decision is still acted on and logged as a violation, assuming that a late mitigation can still help; to drop late actions instead, the check would move before the transmission. Our timing xApp (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) differs in three ways: at every report it scores a held-out test window for every UE, as if each report completed a window; it sends a control message after every report, so that $t_{\mathrm{act}}$ is always measured; and it records the full loop instead of applying the check.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Budget-aware decision loop of a detection xApp}
\label{alg:xapp}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require indication stream $\mathcal{S}$, model $f_{\theta}$, threshold $\tau$, in-xApp budget $B_{x} < B$
\Ensure E2 control actions; violation count $v$; latency record
\State $\mathcal{W} \gets$ empty per-UE window map; $v \gets 0$
\ForAll{indication $m$ arriving on $\mathcal{S}$}
  \State $t_{0} \gets \textsc{Now}()$; \; $u \gets \textsc{UEOf}(m)$
  \State $\mathcal{W}[u] \gets \textsc{Append}(\mathcal{W}[u], m)$
  \If{$|\mathcal{W}[u]| = 16$} \Comment{non-overlapping windows}
    \State $x \gets \textsc{Features}(\mathcal{W}[u])$; \; $\mathcal{W}[u] \gets$ empty
    \State $\hat{p} \gets f_{\theta}(x)$
    \If{$\hat{p} \ge \tau$}
       \State $\textsc{SendE2}(\textsc{EncodeControl}(u, \hat{p}))$ \Comment{even if late}
    \EndIf
    \State $\ell \gets \textsc{Now}() - t_{0}$
    \If{$\ell > B_{x}$}
       \State $v \gets v + 1$ \Comment{deadline violation, recorded}
    \EndIf
    \State $\textsc{RecordLatency}(\ell)$ \Comment{every decision}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All measurements ran on one workstation (13th Gen Intel Core i9-13900H, 14 cores, 20 threads, 47.6\,GB of memory, Windows 11 Pro build 26120) with Python 3.11.9, scikit-learn 1.8.0, XGBoost 3.2.0, NumPy 2.3.5, and ONNX Runtime 1.30.0; the sequence models were trained with PyTorch 2.14 on the CPU in a separate environment. The full package list ships with the results.

For the latency loop we built FlexRIC~\cite{schmidt2021flexric} v2.0.0 in WSL2 (Ubuntu, Linux kernel 6.18) on the same machine and connected its near-real-time RIC over SCTP to FlexRIC's emulated gNB agent. Our C xApp subscribes to E2SM-KPM reports with 16 measurement items per UE (one radio record), scores every UE of a report with ONNX Runtime 1.30.0 through its C API, answers each report with one E2SM-RC control message (radio bearer control, QoS flow mapping, the only control the emulated node implements), and waits for the acknowledgment. The emulated node reports synthetic values for 0 to \RicUEsMax{} UEs, so the xApp scores held-out test windows in their place; before timing, the C code reproduced the Python scores on all 589 test windows to within $4\times10^{-7}$. Delivery time comes from the timestamp the node writes into the KPM header, since all processes share one clock. No radio, real E2 node, or container platform took part, and all other latency figures were measured without a RIC.

The radio protocol comparison uses 20 splits with two model seeds each, and the other experiments 20 splits with one. For the flow-level bootstrap intervals and every result on \DB{} without the benign copies, we re-ran the first 10 splits deterministically with confusion counts per capture file (runtime limited the re-run to 10). Calibration uses 10 splits of a three-way source split into training, calibration, and test groups. We declare an attack prevalence of $\pi = 0.002$ and a benign arrival rate of $\lambda_{b} = 240{,}000$ flows per hour. Neither comes from an operator, and we found no measured source for $\pi$; Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate} gives the prevalence at which the precision conclusion would change. On the radio layer, false alerts are counted per benign UE-hour. Our rate of about 67 flows per second is below the several hundred per second that Jin et al. extrapolate from measured radio-bearer arrivals at a heavily loaded base station of a large LTE network~\cite{jin2013softcell}, so our alert volumes probably understate those of an edge site serving several cells.

Latency outside the RIC is measured per call with a monotonic clock and garbage collection enabled, over 20,000 calls after 1,000 warm-up calls, with one thread per library unless stated otherwise, and every quantile has a distribution-free confidence interval. To time flow-level feature construction, we use two Python flow exporters of our own with the same flow semantics, a per-packet reference implementation and a vectorized version that extracts header fields in bulk, on the three DDoS captures of \DA{}.

\subsection{Evaluation-Protocol Sensitivity}
\label{sec:res:protocol}

Table~\ref{tab:leakage} and Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage} compare three protocols on the radio layer. Against a session-disjoint split, a random split raises macro-$F_1$ by \LeakMin{} to \LeakMax{} across the six architectures, \LeakAvg{} on average, and the gain is positive in at least \LeakSeedsMin{} of the 20 splits for every architecture. The input-blind baselines gain nothing, so class composition alone does not move macro-$F_1$ between protocols, which is why we compare protocols in macro-$F_1$ on this layer. The corrected interval of the average gain is [\LeakAvgNbLo, \LeakAvgNbHi] ($p = \LeakAvgPnb$): with thirty sessions we cannot pin down the size of the effect, and no single architecture's gain survives Holm correction. An uncorrected paired $t$-test would have given $p = \LeakAvgPt$, which shows how much the naive test understates the variance.

The gain is not caused by test categories missing from training: every test category appeared in training in all 20 session-disjoint splits. The category-stratified protocol guarantees this by design and gains \LeakStratMin{} to \LeakStratMax{}, which we do not count as extra evidence because it pushes rare-category sessions into a harder test set. A model-free probe points to a mechanism. Under a random split, \NNSame\% of the test windows have their nearest training window in the same session (\NNSameMin\% to \NNSameMax\% across splits), although windows share no records; a neighbor drawn at random from same-category training windows would share the session \NNChanceCat\% of the time, and one from all training windows \NNChanceAny\%. The 1-NN lookup reaches a macro-$F_1$ of \NNoneRandom{}, against \NNoneRun{} under a session-disjoint split (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). As every radio label is a session label, knowing the session gives the label, and a random split lets any model use this. The probe shows that session recognition is available and sufficient, not that the trained models rely on it.

Under the session-disjoint split, the lookup beats LR and DT and comes within \NNgapBest{} of the best supervised architecture, so on this layer the learned models add little to retrieving similar windows. The two sequence models reach \SeqRandomMin{} to \SeqRandomMax{} under a random split and \SeqRDmin{} to \SeqRDmax{} under a session-disjoint split, below the 1-NN lookup; their gain of \SeqGainMin{} to \SeqGainMax{} has corrected intervals that exclude zero for both (Holm-adjusted $p \le \SeqPholmMax{}$), helped by the smaller Holm correction of a family of two.

\subsection{Time Order and Benign Sessions}
\label{sec:res:drift}

The radio layer spans \DriftDays{} days, but its sessions were captured in blocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:benign}): nine benign sessions on days 0 to 6, the five attack categories on days 47 to 53, and a last benign session on day 53. Split by order, whole attack categories end up held out: for training fractions of 0.4 to 0.7, the latest sessions contain \DvAuditUnseenMin{} to \DvAuditUnseenMax{} attack categories never seen in training, plus one benign session. Such a split measures the detection of unseen categories, not temporal drift, and we do not report it as a drift estimate.

To hold category coverage fixed, we held out the latest session of every category, benign included, and compared the result with \DvNctrl{} control draws that each hold out a random session per category. The latest sessions give a balanced accuracy of \DvFwdBAmin{} to \DvFwdBAmax{}, close to chance, against control means of \DvCtrlBAmin{} to \DvCtrlBAmax{}, yet \DvFwdPctMin\% to \DvFwdPctMax\% of the control draws score as low. The earliest give \DvRevBAmin{} to \DvRevBAmax{}, which \DvRevPctMin\% to \DvRevPctMax\% of the control draws match or beat. With so few sessions, the design cannot separate an effect of time order from ordinary variation between sessions.

Much of that variation depends on which benign session is held out (Fig.~\ref{fig:benign}). Averaged over the six architectures, \BenLowN{} of the ten benign sessions give a false positive rate of at most \BenLowMax{}, session \BenMidSession{} gives \BenMidFPR{}, and sessions \BenEarlyHighSessions{} (days \BenEarlyHighDays{}) give \BenEarlyHighMin{} and \BenEarlyHighMax{}, so one first-week session is flagged more often than the last benign session, the only one from the attack period (\BenLateFPR{}, or \BenLateFA{} false alerts per benign UE-hour). With ten benign sessions, a change over time cannot be told apart from this heterogeneity, and a detector's false positive rate on an unseen benign session of this testbed cannot be predicted from the sessions it was tested on.

\subsection{The Target Corpus: Labels and a Published Result}
\label{sec:res:target}

\emph{Label conflicts.} For half of \DB{} the labels do not follow from the flow records: of the \NDbFlows{} flows, \LcShareAll\% (\LcFlowsAll) match a flow with the opposite label in every field except the row index, \texttt{Seq}, and \texttt{Offset} (Table~\ref{tab:conflict}, Fig.~\ref{fig:conflict}). All sit in the two UDP-flood files, \LcBenignFileConf\% of file~\LcBenignFile{} (station~1) and \LcAttackFileConf\% of file~\LcAttackFile{} (station~2). Each of the \LcTwinAttack{} flows labeled UDP flood in file~\LcAttackFile{} has a copy labeled benign in file~\LcBenignFile{} that agrees in every content field, and \LcTwinEqual{} of the \LcTwinRecords{} distinct records involved occur equally often in both files. The other \LcOwnConf{} conflicting flows are floods of station~1's own attacker, labeled attack in file~\LcBenignFile{}, with the same content as benign-labeled copies. The copies make up \LcBenignTwinPct\% of the benign class.

Which label is right? The full-field release carries addresses and lines up with the combined file row for row. All \LcTwinBenign{} benign copies are UDP flows from \LcAddrSrc{} to \LcAddrDst{}, the host pair, protocol, and time window of the flood labeled attack in file~\LcAttackFile{}, whereas the attack labeled in file~\LcBenignFile{} comes from another host, \LcAddrOwnSrc{}, aimed at the same address. Station~1's capture thus appears to have recorded station~2's flood too, labeling only its own attacker; how is not documented, so this reading is our inference from the addresses. We therefore treat the copies as attack traffic with a wrong label and, rather than change published labels, report operating points on \DB{} (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}) and the transfer results of Section~\ref{sec:res:transfer} with and without them; Tables~\ref{tab:arms} and~\ref{tab:calibration} and Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability} use all flows.

These conflicts cap any classifier that reads only record content: even on the flows it was built from, the best function of the \LcNFeat{} content fields reaches a balanced accuracy of \LcCeilNative{}, and of the 18 shared columns \LcCeilShared{}, where DT, XGB, HGB, and the MLP land on a random split of \DB{} (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}). With \texttt{Seq} and \texttt{Offset}, almost every record becomes unique and the ceiling rises to \LcCeilPos{}.

\emph{References within the target.} To separate the cost of transfer from the limits of \DB{} itself, we trained and tested the six architectures on \DB{} alone (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}; one to three training samples per protocol, no intervals). A random split gives \TgtRandShBAmin{} to \TgtRandShBAmax{} in the shared space, the upper end being the ceiling above, and \TgtRandShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtRandShCleanBAmax{} once the benign copies leave the test set. Held-out capture files give \TgtFileShBAmin{} to \TgtFileShBAmax{} in the shared space and \TgtFileNatBAmin{} to \TgtFileNatBAmax{} with native features, though no held-out set contained a UDP-flood file. Across base stations, balanced accuracy drops to \TgtSiteABShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteABShBAmax{} from station~1 to station~2 and to \TgtSiteBAShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShBAmax{} the other way. In the second direction a model flags the benign-labeled copies of station~1 and scores \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmax{} once they leave the test set; in the first, the copies sit in the training data, so that a model trained with them labeled benign misses the floods of station~2 is our inference, not a tested result. Where labels follow from record content, the shared space separates the classes of \DB{} almost perfectly, so the shared features are not what limits transfer to \DB{}.

\subsection{Transfer Across Corpora}
\label{sec:res:transfer}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer} give the transfer from \DA{} to \DB{}. In macro-$F_1$ every supervised model loses \TrFoneDropMin{} to \TrFoneDropMax{}, but so does the feature-blind majority baseline (\TrMajDrop{}), simply because \DB{} has a lower attack prevalence; net of it, the loss is \TrDiDmin{} to \TrDiDmax{}, none significant after correction. Macro-$F_1$ is the wrong yardstick here, and the low source macro-$F_1$ values also reflect the \PrevDa\% attack prevalence of \DA{} rather than weak detectors. Balanced accuracy, which prevalence leaves untouched, falls from \TrSrcBAmin{}--\TrSrcBAmax{} on held-out source data to \TrTgtBAmin{}--\TrTgtBAmax{} on \DB{}, a drop of \TrBAdropMin{} to \TrBAdropMax{} that is significant for \TrSigBA{} of the six architectures after correction, positive in at least \TrBAseedsMin{} of the 20 splits for every architecture, and \TrBAdropAvg{} [\TrBAdropAvgNbLo, \TrBAdropAvgNbHi] on average. The trivial baselines do not move, and target ROC-AUC lies between \TrTgtAUCmin{} and \TrTgtAUCmax{}.

The detectors stay above chance on the target, but not by much: average target balanced accuracy is \TrTgtBAavg{}, and the corrected interval excludes 0.5 for \TrChanceSig{} of the six architectures (\TrChanceSigHolm{} after Holm correction). In macro-$F_1$ the stratified sampler scores \TrStratFloor{} and a fair coin \TrFairCoin{}, a difference due only to the prior. LR transfers best on both prevalence-independent measures (balanced accuracy \TrLRtgtBA{}, ROC-AUC \TrLRtgtAUC{}), while the MLP, strongest on held-out source data (\TrMLPsrcBA{}), reaches \TrMLPtgtBA{}, among the lowest. With six architectures, the rank correlation between source and target balanced accuracy (\RhoBA{}, $p=\RhoBAP$) is too imprecise to tell whether source performance predicts target performance, and the MLP shows that the best source model can be among the worst after transfer. The macro-$F_1$ rank correlation (\RhoFone{}, $p=\RhoFoneP$) mixes discrimination with each model's operating point under each corpus's class balance and is equally imprecise. Our results agree in direction with Abraheem and Edhirig~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional} (0.50 to 0.56 per attack family from NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD to 5G-NIDD); ours are pooled over attack types, so the sizes are not directly comparable.

\emph{Effect of the target labels.} Some of this loss comes from the label conflict. A detector that learned to recognize a UDP flood on \DA{} finds the floods in file~\LcAttackFile{} and, by the same rule, flags the benign-labeled copies in file~\LcBenignFile{} (the five nonlinear architectures flag \NetCopyFlagOthMin{} to \NetCopyFlagMax{} of them, LR none). Without the copies (first 10 splits), target balanced accuracy is \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{} (\TrCleanOthMin{} to \TrCleanOthMax{} nonlinear, \TrCleanLR{} for LR), and the loss against held-out source data shrinks to \TrCleanDropMin{} to \TrCleanDropMax{}, significant for \TrCleanSig{} of the six after correction. The conflicts reverse the ranking: LR looks best on all flows and is worst on the flows with consistent labels. Even there, transfer falls \TrCleanShortMin{} to \TrCleanShortMax{} short of the in-target reference on a random split (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}), and the stricter capture-file reference is higher still (\TgtFileShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtFileShCleanBAmax{}).

At $\tau = 0.5$, no architecture both keeps the benign traffic of \DB{} clear and catches most of its attacks. LR keeps a benign specificity of \FwdLRspec{} but catches only \FwdLRdos{} of the DoS traffic, while the tree models and the MLP catch more attacks and flag \FwdEnsFlagMin{} to \FwdEnsFlagMax{} of the benign-labeled traffic, which includes the flood copies; without them, their false positive rate is \FwdEnsCleanFPRmin{} to \FwdEnsCleanFPRmax{}.

The reverse direction looks different (per-model values are in the repository). Balanced accuracy on \DA{} reaches \TrRevTgtBAmin{} to \TrRevTgtBAmax{}, and the loss against the source reference (source minus target, optimistic because it comes from a random split) ranges from \TrRevBAdropMin{} to \TrRevBAdropMax{}: LR gains \TrRevLRgain{}, XGB, HGB, and the MLP change little, and DT and RF lose \TrRevTreeLossMin{} to \TrRevTreeLossMax{}, the changes of LR, DT, and RF being significant after Holm correction. Macro-$F_1$ still falls sharply, because false negatives swamp the small benign class of \DA{} (\PrevDa\% attacks). Why a model trained on the narrower \DB{} separates \DA{} better than the reverse we have not tested; one possibility is that the larger benign class of \DB{}, although \LcBenignTwinPct\% of it are flood copies, teaches a better model of benign behavior. Categories that \DB{} lacks are detected unevenly: the ensembles and the MLP recall at most \RevWebEnsMax{} of the web attacks but \RevBFmin{} to \RevBFmax{} of the brute-force attacks.

\subsection{Composition of the Transfer Gap}
\label{sec:res:anatomy}

\emph{Separability.} The domain classifier (Section~\ref{sec:detectors}) tells \DA{} from \DB{} flows with a balanced accuracy of \DomShared{} on the 18 shared columns and \DomRobust{} on the nine exporter-robust ones. The largest shifts are in the protocol mix (most \DA{} flows are TCP, most \DB{} flows UDP), the direction ratios, and the packet counts (Fig.~\ref{fig:shift}). The robust columns shift as much as the others ($\bar{W}_1$ of \WoneRobust{} against \WoneNonrobust), pointing to a difference in traffic composition that no exporter would remove; whether flow segmentation also differs, the next test addresses.

\emph{A common exporter.} The raw captures of \DB{} are public, so we re-extracted them with Zeek (version \ZkVersion{}), the exporter family of \DA{}, and mapped the records with the function used for \DA{}. In every capture file the published attack flows belong to one host pair, and labeling a flow as attack when its host pair is an attack pair of the same file reproduces \ZkRuleAgreePct\% of the published labels on the Argus flows, so we carried these labels over to the \ZkFlows{} Zeek flows (\ZkAttackPct\% attacks). Zeek, which segments traffic differently (Section~\ref{sec:shared}), produces about a third as many flows, with a higher attack share; we did not analyze which flows account for the difference. The flood copies reappear in the Zeek output (\ZkCopies{} flows), so they are in the captures, not an artifact of the Argus export. We reran the transfer on the \ZkSeeds{} splits of the Argus results without the copies, with identical trained models, so that only the export of the target changes.

The gap does not close. On all flows, balanced accuracy falls from \ZkArgBAmin{}--\ZkArgBAmax{} with Argus to \ZkBAmin{}--\ZkBAmax{} with Zeek, and the paired difference over splits (\ZkDiffMin{} to \ZkDiffMax{}) has a 95\% paired $t$-interval below zero for all \ZkSigNeg{} architectures, uncorrected for split overlap since both arms use the same trained models. Without the copies, five architectures move by \ZkCleanOthDiffMin{} to \ZkCleanOthDiffMax{}, none significantly, and LR loses \ZkCleanLRloss{}, while the false positive rate rises for every architecture (\ZkCleanFPRmin{}--\ZkCleanFPRmax{} against \ZkCleanArgFPRmin{}--\ZkCleanArgFPRmax{} with Argus). Two explanations remain, the compositional difference and differences between our Zeek configuration and the undocumented one behind \DA{}, such as timeouts or single-packet handling (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The Zeek result cannot separate them; the robust-column shift favors composition without ruling out the second.

\emph{Model families and adaptation.} None of three modifications run on the same splits produces a detector that transfers (Table~\ref{tab:arms}). The nine exporter-robust columns alone lift held-out source balanced accuracy to \RobSrcBAmin{} to \RobSrcBAmax{} but leave the target at \RobBAmin{} to \RobBAmax{}, no better than chance, which fits the compositional shift in these columns. CORAL, which reads unlabeled target features, gives \CoralBAmin{} to \CoralBAmax{}; it helps only the MLP (\CoralMLPtgtBA{} against \TrMLPtgtBA{}) and raises the target false positive rate to \CoralFPRmin{} to \CoralFPRmax{}. The novelty detectors, trained on benign traffic alone, already fail on held-out source data, where the autoencoder falls below chance, likely (we did not test this) because floods and scans look like short benign flows in this feature space.

\subsection{A Third Corpus}
\label{sec:res:third}

To see whether these results hold beyond one pair of corpora, we added \DC{} as a second target, which changes the site, the flow exporter, and the vantage point together. Its labels are nearly consistent: only \ThConfShare\% of its flows sit on records that also occur with the opposite label, and the ceiling on balanced accuracy in the shared columns is \ThCeilShared{}. Trained and tested on \DC{}, the six architectures reach \ThRefShRandBAmin{} to \ThRefShRandBAmax{} on a random split, and \ThRefShLofoBAmin{} to \ThRefShLofoBAmax{} when the capture file of one attack type is held out so that this type is never seen in training.

For transfer from \DA{} (Table~\ref{tab:third}, 20 splits), balanced accuracy on \DC{} ranges from \ThATgtBAmin{} to \ThATgtBAmax{} (ROC-AUC \ThATgtAUCmin{} to \ThATgtAUCmax), close to the in-target reference that holds out an attack type (\ThRefShLofoBAmin{} to \ThRefShLofoBAmax{}), the fairer comparison because transfer, too, faces an unseen attack type, the PFCP attack. Every architecture detects the SYN flood, which training contained; recall is \ThAIcmpMin{} to \ThAIcmpMax{} on the ICMP flood and \ThAPfcpMin{} to \ThAPfcpMax{} on the PFCP attack. Most errors come from the benign traffic of the new site: the false positive rate is \ThAFPRmin{} to \ThAFPRmax{}, and up to \ThABgFlagMax{} of the core network's own signaling is flagged. The loss against held-out source data (\ThADropMin{} to \ThADropMax{}) varies so much between splits that none of it is significant after correction. From \DB{} (10 splits), balanced accuracy spans \ThBTgtBAmin{} to \ThBTgtBAmax{}, ROC-AUC falls as low as \ThBTgtAUCmin{} (a ranking inverted on \DC{}), and the loss is significant for \ThBSig{} of the six. At $\pi = 0.002$, the best operational precision at recall above 10\% is \ThAPPVbest{} from \DA{} and \ThBPPVbest{} from \DB{}, and no architecture passes the deployability test in either direction. Three findings thus repeat at a third site with a third exporter and nearly consistent labels: the SYN flood is caught, much benign traffic is flagged, and operational precision stays below 0.1.

\subsection{Alert Burden}
\label{sec:res:alerts}

Table~\ref{tab:pooled} gives pooled operating points at $\tau=0.5$ for four evaluation sets. On the radio layer, the six detectors have a pooled false positive rate of \RadFPRmin{} to \RadFPRmax{} (\RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} false alerts per benign UE-hour) and, at $\pi=0.002$, an operational precision of \RadPPVmin{} to \RadPPVmax{}, against a corpus precision of \RadPrecMin{} to \RadPrecMax{}. These estimates are imprecise, because the false positive rate depends on which benign sessions enter the test set (Fig.~\ref{fig:benign}): the bootstrap interval of the pooled rate spans \RadCbFPRlo{} to \RadCbFPRhi{} across architectures (\RadCbFAlo{} to \RadCbFAhi{} false alerts per benign UE-hour), and that of operational precision \RadCbPPVlo{} to \RadCbPPVhi{}. Thirty sessions cannot pin down the radio alert burden; they show only that it depends on the benign conditions a detector meets.

On the flow layer the alert volumes are also large. The false positive rate is \NetSrcFPRmin{} to \NetSrcFPRmax{} on held-out \DA{} flows, or \NetSrcFAmin{} to \NetSrcFAmax{} false alerts per hour at $\lambda_b = 240{,}000$ benign flows per hour, and \NetTgtFPRmin{} to \NetTgtFPRmax{} on all flows of \DB{} (\NetTgtFAmin{} to \NetTgtFAmax{} per hour). Without the benign copies it is \NetCleanFPRmin{} to \NetCleanFPRmax{}, still \NetCleanFAmin{} to \NetCleanFAmax{} false alerts per hour, at an operational precision of \NetCleanPPVmin{} to \NetCleanPPVmax{}.

On \DB{}, raising the threshold barely improves operational precision (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}b). With the best threshold picked on the evaluation data, and recall above 10\%, the upper bounds are \NetSrcPPVbest{} on held-out \DA{} flows and, on \DB{}, \NetTgtPPVbest{} on all flows or \NetCleanPPVbest{} without the copies. On the radio layer a higher threshold does help (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}a), up to \RadPPVbest{}, but for \RadCbBestHiN{} of the six architectures the bootstrap interval reaches 1.0: a resample that leaves out the benign sessions flagged almost entirely can leave thresholds with no false positive. The ratio of corpus to operational precision is pure arithmetic in $\pi$, from \RadCollapseLowPi{} at $\pi = 10^{-4}$ to \RadCollapseHighPi{} at 0.5, so an operational precision means something only with its assumed prevalence.

\emph{Estimation of operational precision.} A split without a false positive yields $\mathrm{PPV}=1$ whatever its recall, so averaging PPV over splits rewards the architecture that draws the most such splits: on the radio layer the ratio between the best and worst architecture is \EstPooled{} with pooled counts, \EstMedian{} as a median over splits, and \EstMean{} as a mean. We use pooled counts throughout.

\subsection{Calibration and Prior Shift}
\label{sec:res:calibration}

Under transfer the scores are poorly calibrated (Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}). We fitted three calibrators on a group-disjoint source calibration fold (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}); whether one can move the reachable operating points depends on whether it keeps the order of the scores. Temperature scaling does, changing ROC-AUC by at most $\CalTempMax$. Platt scaling is monotone too, but its slope can be negative: for DT it inverted the ranking in \CalPlattFlipSeeds{} of \CalSeeds{} splits (ROC-AUC shift \CalPlattFlip), because the tree's scores carried no ranking information on the calibration fold (ROC-AUC \PlattCalAUC{} on \PlattCalGroups{} held-out addresses) but ranked the test fold well (\PlattTestAUC), so the fitted slope of \PlattSlope{} inverted it; the small calibration fold is to blame, not Platt scaling. Isotonic regression, monotone but piecewise constant, merges scores into ties and yields the ROC convex hull on its fitting data~\cite{fawcett2007pav}; on the target it lowered ROC-AUC by up to \CalIsoDownMax{} and raised it by at most \CalIsoUpMax{}. No calibrator raises target balanced accuracy for any architecture (changes of \CalBAchgMin{} to \CalBAchgMax{}). Fitted on a source with \PrevDa\% attacks, the calibrators pull the scores toward that prior, and the target false positive rate at $\tau = 0.5$ rises from \CalFPRrawMin{}--\CalFPRrawMax{} to \CalFPRcalMin{}--\CalFPRcalMax{} after Platt or isotonic calibration.

The usual remedy for prior shift is to rescale the scores to the target prior~\cite{saerens2002adjusting}, which a deployment does not know. Expectation maximization~\cite{saerens2002adjusting} and black-box shift estimation~\cite{lipton2018bbse} estimate it from unlabeled data, assuming that only the class balance changes, which fails here: for the published target prior of \PriorTrue{}, the mean estimates per architecture run from \PriorMeanMin{} to \PriorMeanMax{}, and \PriorFracExtreme\% of the estimates fall below 0.05 or above 0.95. Even an oracle that rescales with the published prior does not beat the raw scores. The detectors are limited by discrimination on the target (ROC-AUC \TrTgtAUCmin{} to \TrTgtAUCmax{}), not by calibration.

\subsection{Implications for O-RAN Practice}
\label{sec:disc:practice}

\emph{Data path.} E2SM-KPM delivers aggregated measurements once per reporting period, not packets, so a window-level detector can live inside the RIC, whereas a flow-level detector needs an exporter near the user plane (in the central unit or at the user-plane function) and a way to move its records to the xApp, such as a custom E2 service model or the non-real-time data collection path. That transport adds to $t_{\mathrm{ind}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:latency}, which our RIC loop measures for KPM reports only.

\emph{Detection delay.} Computation is a small part of detection time: a window-level decision waits for its 16\,s window to end, a flow-level one for the flow record, which for a long connection means an idle or active timeout. Computational latency should be reported with the KPM reporting period and flow timeouts.

\emph{Acting on alerts.} An E2SM-RC action such as isolating or steering a UE hits a falsely accused UE as hard as a guilty one. At the rates in Table~\ref{tab:pooled}, automatic mitigation on the radio layer would act against an average benign UE \RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} times an hour, far more often in some benign sessions than in others (Fig.~\ref{fig:benign}). Alerts of this quality can inform an operator, or a policy set through the A1 interface of the non-real-time RIC, but should not drive automatic control, and for such detectors the 10\,ms budget matters less than detection quality: latency binds only for a detector whose alerts are good enough to act on automatically.

\emph{Admission and monitoring.} The deployability test could serve as an admission check at a new site, given labeled traffic from it. Afterwards, unsupervised shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing} can flag traffic that departs from the training distribution; as a supervised classifier separates our corpora with a balanced accuracy of \DomShared{}, such a monitor would likely detect this shift, though we did not test one.

\subsection{Relation to Reported Results}

The 5G-NIDD results we cite~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv,ilias2024moe5g} are in-distribution scores, and under the same split ours are as high: \PubRzAccMin\% to \PubRzAccMax\% accuracy for the reproduced pipeline and \ThRefShRandBAmin{} to \ThRefShRandBAmax{} balanced accuracy inside \DC{}. Setting such numbers beside transfer results would compare protocols, not detectors. In our transfer tests, a held-out score under a random split did not predict performance on another corpus: on the radio layer it rose with session recognition, on 5G-NIDD it rested on record-position fields, and the best source model was among the worst after transfer. The reporting requirements of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} list the quantities that changed our conclusions.

\section{Reproducibility and Ethics}
\label{sec:repro}

Code, configuration, seeded splits, per-split results, and the generators for every table, figure, and in-text number are at \textcolor{blue}{\url{https://github.com/adeliusa486/oran-ids-deployability}}, where one script regenerates and checks every number or re-runs each experiment from the SHA-256-verified raw corpora (\texttt{REPRODUCE.md}); it builds FlexRIC, the xApp, and Zeek without administrator rights on Linux or WSL2. \DA{}~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab} and \DB{}~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd}, including the full-field release and the GTP-removed captures, are distributed by their authors under CC-BY-4.0. \DC{} is available from its authors' repository~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}, which states no license, so we do not redistribute it. All traffic comes from testbed devices and test networks, and no personal data was processed. Showing when detectors fail carries a modest dual-use risk, which we judge smaller than the benefit to operators of knowing that reported accuracy need not carry over to their networks.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Flow exporters.} Zeek, Argus, and NFStream produced \DA{}, \DB{}, and \DC{}. Re-extracting \DB{} with Zeek did not close the gap (Section~\ref{sec:res:anatomy}), but the Zeek version and configuration behind \DA{} are undocumented, so our export may still differ from it in timeouts or in the handling of single-packet flows. The Zeek flows take their labels through the host pairs of each capture; the rule reproduces every published label on the Argus flows, but would give the attack label to a Zeek flow whose host pair carries both attack and benign traffic within one capture. \DC{} was not re-extracted.

\emph{Target labels.} We treat the benign flows of \DB{} that are content copies of UDP-flood records (\LcBenignTwinPct\% of the benign class) as mislabeled because their addresses point to the attacker at the other base station (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}), an inference from the addresses alone, so every operating point on \DB{} appears with and without them. The recovered capture files match the per-station files of the full-field release in row count and attacking host (all \LcSiteFiles{} files of station~1).

\emph{Radio capture schedule.} Nine of the ten benign radio sessions were recorded six weeks before the first attack session, mostly with one UE, and every radio label is a session label, so a radio detector may learn capture periods as well as attacks. The one benign session from the attack period is flagged almost entirely, but so are two early sessions (Fig.~\ref{fig:benign}), and ten benign sessions are too few to decide whether the detectors learned capture periods or the benign sessions simply differ from each other.

\emph{Number of corpora.} Three corpora show that the transfer gap recurs, not how it varies across sites. The third is a 5G core testbed, not an O-RAN deployment, with only flood attacks, one control-plane attack, and three capture files, too few for cluster-based intervals. We found no other O-RAN corpus with paired radio telemetry.

\emph{Latency.} All latency figures come from one workstation without CPU isolation, and the RIC loop used an emulated E2 node over loopback SCTP, with no radio and no network between sites. It measures the processing cost of the RIC software path, which also depends on power management, not the delay of a deployment, and covers window-level detectors only. CPU and memory under load were not measured.

\emph{Operational parameters and the deployability test.} The prevalence $\pi = 0.002$ and the rate $\lambda_b$ are declared, not measured on an operational network, and we found no measured source for $\pi$. Only $\pi$ enters operational precision, and alert counts scale linearly with $\lambda_b$. The test's bounds are our own choice; Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate} shows the verdict under other bounds and prevalences, but an operator may weigh recall, precision, and alert volume differently, and the test ignores detection delay and the cost of a missed attack.

\emph{Sample size.} The radio layer has 30 sessions, each benign or from one attack category, so session and category are confounded, and the protocol and temporal analyses detect only large effects. With ten benign sessions, the radio false positive rate and operational precision carry wide intervals (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}). The transfer statistics vary the training set over 20 splits but test on one fixed target corpus, so they miss the variability across other target corpora.

\emph{Scope.} We study binary detection only. Multi-class attribution, feature attribution~\cite{lundberg2017shap}, and the fusion of radio and flow features, which needs the raw archives~\cite{fard2026crosslayer} because the summary files cannot be joined, remain open. Three attack categories of \DA{} are missing from \DB{} and can be tested in one direction only.

\section{Conclusion and Reporting Requirements}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A held-out score on one corpus says little about how a detection xApp will behave on data from another site. On the radio layer of NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD, a random split raised macro-$F_1$ by \LeakAvg{} on average (95\% interval \LeakAvgNbLo{} to \LeakAvgNbHi{}), and a model-free lookup gained about as much, consistent with the recognition of capture sessions. Half of 5G-NIDD lies on records that occur under both labels, the addresses of the benign copies point to the attacker at the other base station, and the published 99.9\% depends on the record-position fields that tell the copies apart. On flows with consistent labels, transferred detectors reach a balanced accuracy of \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}, below what the target corpus itself allows, although the loss against held-out source data is significant for only \TrCleanSig{} architecture; on a third corpus they catch the SYN flood but flag much benign traffic, with no significant loss against source data. At the declared prevalence of 0.002, no transferred detector reaches an operational precision of 0.1 at any threshold with recall above 10\%. Only window-level detectors meet the latency term, at \RicLoopTenPnnMin{} to \RicLoopTenPnnMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile through a FlexRIC loop with an emulated E2 node on one host, a measurement rather than a conformance result that leaves out the detection delay of a 16\,s window or a flow timeout. We therefore propose that any study claiming a deployable O-RAN detection capability report at least the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item performance on an independently collected corpus in a prevalence-invariant metric (balanced accuracy or ROC-AUC), beside input-blind baselines and a group-disjoint reference trained inside that corpus;
\item the split protocol and group key, group-disjoint results with intervals that account for overlap between resampled splits, and the category coverage of any time-based split;
\item the benign false positive rate with its unit, the resulting alert rate, and the operational precision at a stated base rate, from pooled confusion counts with cluster-resampling intervals;
\item the operating threshold, how it was chosen, and the recall it gives up;
\item tail latency from indication to control acknowledgment, with stages, implementation, reporting period, and host, measured through a RIC and, before any conformance claim, with a real E2 node;
\item evidence that the labels are a function of the features used, with the share of identical records under opposite labels, and that no record-position or identifier field is a feature.
\end{enumerate}
