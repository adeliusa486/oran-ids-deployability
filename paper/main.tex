\begin{abstract}
Intrusion detectors for 5G and O-RAN traffic are frequently reported to exceed 99\% accuracy on a random split of a single corpus. This paper examines what such results predict about deployment, using NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD, 5G-NIDD, six architectures, two input-blind baselines, and a ladder of increasingly strict protocols. A random split raises radio-layer macro-$F_1$ by \LeakAvg{} (95\% interval \LeakAvgNbLo{} to \LeakAvgNbHi{}; significant for two sequence models), and a nearest-neighbor lookup gains \NNGain{}, consistent with the recognition of capture sessions. Because the radio capture is ordered by scenario, a time-disjoint split excludes entire attack categories from training. When category coverage is held fixed, temporal order cannot be distinguished from session-to-session variation, and the false positive rate on a held-out benign session ranges from 0.00 to \BenHighMax{}. In 5G-NIDD, \LcBenignTwinPct\% of the benign flows duplicate flood records labeled as attacks elsewhere, limiting balanced accuracy to \LcCeilNative{}. The 99.9\% accuracy reported by the dataset authors is reproducible but depends on two record-position fields, without which accuracy decreases to \PubOneNoposAccMax\%. On 5G-NIDD flows with consistent labels, transferred detectors achieve a balanced accuracy of \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}, below the in-target reference, and none satisfies a deployability test that the published pipeline satisfies on its own split. A third corpus exhibits the same behavior. At a prevalence of 0.002, operational precision does not exceed \NetCleanPPVbest{} at any threshold that retains recall above 10\%. Averaging precision across folds inflates the best-to-worst ratio between architectures from \EstPooled{} to \EstMean{}. The paper concludes with reporting requirements for deployability claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) architectures disaggregate the base station into radio, distributed, and central units that are connected through open interfaces, and they introduce a RAN Intelligent Controller (RIC) that hosts third-party control applications~\cite{polese2023understanding,abdalla2022toward,niknam2022intelligent}. Because the near-real-time RIC operates close to the cells it controls~\cite{bonati2021intelligence,polese2023understanding}, it has access to the telemetry of every device served by those cells. Compromised devices, many of which are IoT devices, are a well-documented source of volumetric attacks~\cite{antonakakis2017mirai,kolias2017ddos}. A detection application (xApp) placed in the RIC control loop is therefore an attractive defensive option. Platforms for developing xApps are available~\cite{polese2023coloran,schmidt2021flexric}, and detectors that operate within or alongside the O-RAN control loop have already been demonstrated~\cite{wen2024spector,scalingi2024detran}.

Detectors for 5G and O-RAN traffic are typically evaluated on a random held-out split of a single dataset, and the reported accuracy or $F_1$ score commonly exceeds 99\%. For 5G-NIDD, one of the two corpora used in this study, the dataset authors report binary accuracies between 99.85\% and 99.95\% for four of five classifiers~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv}, and subsequent work reports a weighted $F_1$ score of up to 99.95\%~\cite{ilias2024moe5g}. However, whether such a detector can be deployed depends on three properties that a held-out split of the training distribution does not measure. The first is generalization to a deployment that did not contribute training data, and intrusion detectors are known to transfer poorly between independently collected datasets~\cite{layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. The second is the alert burden, since a false positive rate that appears small on a balanced benchmark produces a large volume of alerts once it is multiplied by production flow rates and interpreted against a realistic attack base rate~\cite{axelsson2000baserate,sommer2010outside}. The third is latency relative to the near-real-time control loop, for which the O-RAN E2 specification requires support for latencies between 10\,ms and 1\,s~\cite{etsi_e2gap}. A benchmark score thus indicates how well a detector separates the records of one corpus, whereas deployment concerns its behavior on traffic it was not trained on, at a realistic base rate, and within a control loop. In this paper, benchmark results are treated as evidence about deployment only when an experiment supports that interpretation.

These concerns have been raised in earlier work. The base-rate argument was made by Axelsson~\cite{axelsson2000baserate} and the closed-world critique by Sommer and Paxson~\cite{sommer2010outside}. Common pitfalls of machine learning in security were catalogued by Arp et al.~\cite{arp2022dosdonts}, and temporal and spatial bias were formalized by Pendlebury et al.~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}. Transfer between the two corpora used here has been measured by Abraheem and Edhirig~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}, and inference inside a real near-real-time RIC has been measured by Obiuwevwi et al.~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime}. The contribution of this paper is neither a new detector nor the observation that transfer degrades performance. It is instead a single controlled evaluation of one detector family in which these effects are measured jointly, with input-blind baselines evaluated on every corpus, so that their relative magnitudes can be compared and the claims that commonly rely on them can be tested. The specific contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item We evaluate a ladder of evaluation protocols on a single pipeline with six architectures and two input-blind baselines. A model-free nearest-neighbor probe and two sequence models indicate that the random-split gain arises from the recognition of capture sessions (Section~\ref{sec:res:protocol}). A split that fixes category coverage further shows that the radio corpus does not support an estimate of temporal drift and that the false positive rate depends mainly on which benign session is tested (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}).
\item We audit the target corpus and show that half of the 5G-NIDD flows lie on records that occur under both labels and that a published 99.9\% result on this corpus depends on record-position fields. We then evaluate the published pipeline under the same ladder of protocols (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}).
\item We separate the contributions of prior shift, label conflicts, and transfer failure to the cross-corpus gap by means of a prevalence-invariant metric, a difference-in-differences test against the majority baseline, references measured within the target corpus, and evaluations with and without the conflicting records. The transfer experiment is also repeated on a third corpus collected at a different site with a different exporter (Sections~\ref{sec:res:transfer}, \ref{sec:res:anatomy}, and~\ref{sec:res:third}).
\item We report operational precision and alert volume from pooled confusion counts, in explicit units and with cluster-bootstrap intervals, and we show that averaging precision over evaluation folds inflates the apparent difference between architectures by an order of magnitude (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}).
\item We identify which calibration methods can alter the achievable operating points, show that none of them improves these operating points in the present setting, and show that label-free estimation of the target prior fails when the class-conditional distributions shift (Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration}).
\item We decompose decision latency on one host for three implementations, including a compiled runtime (Section~\ref{sec:res:latency}). Finally, we combine generalization, alert burden, and latency into a deployability test, examine how its verdict depends on its bounds, and derive reporting requirements (Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion}).
\end{itemize}

All latency measurements in this paper are emulated, and no RIC is present in the measurement path. In addition, the three corpora were produced by three different flow exporters, so each cross-corpus gap combines a change of deployment with a change of tooling; Section~\ref{sec:res:anatomy} quantifies the effect of this confound for \DA{} and \DB{}. \section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews four bodies of work that are relevant to the present study: the RIC as a platform for detection, the security of O-RAN, datasets and learning methods for network intrusion detection, and the literature on evaluation practice and dataset shift on which our methodology draws.

\subsection{The Near-Real-Time RIC as a Detection Platform}

xApps subscribe to E2 nodes through service models. Among these, E2SM-KPM delivers periodic key performance measurements, and E2SM-RC exposes control actions~\cite{polese2023understanding}. Platforms such as OpenRAN Gym~\cite{bonati2023openrangym}, SCOPE~\cite{bonati2021scope}, ColO-RAN~\cite{polese2023coloran}, and FlexRIC~\cite{schmidt2021flexric}, together with open radio stacks~\cite{nikaein2014oai,gomez2016srslte}, have made the development of experimental xApps practical. Because E2 carries aggregated measurements rather than packets, the data path of a flow-level detector requires separate consideration, which is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice}.

\subsection{Security of Open RAN}

The threat model of the O-RAN Alliance~\cite{etsi_wg11_threat} and several independent analyses~\cite{liyanage2023openran,mimran2022security} identify the RIC and third-party xApps as a new attack surface, and security mechanisms across the O-RAN interfaces have been implemented and evaluated~\cite{groen2025implementing}. With respect to detection, 5G-Spector provides layer-3 attack detection as an O-RAN compliant service~\cite{wen2024spector}, Det-RAN detects attacks from cross-layer data in real time~\cite{scalingi2024detran}, and Fard et al. examine the fusion of radio telemetry and flow records on the same source corpus that is used in this paper~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}. These studies propose or evaluate detectors within a single deployment. The present study asks, instead, what evidence would be needed to show that such a detector remains effective in a different deployment.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Network Intrusion Detection}

A number of IoT-oriented benchmark corpora are available~\cite{koroniotis2019botiot,moustafa2021toniot,ferrag2022edgeiiot,neto2023ciciot,sharafaldin2018cicids}. Cellular corpora are less common. 5G-NIDD was captured on an operational 5G test network~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, and NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD combines flow records with radio telemetry collected on an O-RAN testbed~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}. For 5G-NIDD, the dataset authors report binary accuracies of 99.85\% to 99.95\% for four of five classifiers on a random 70/30 split~\cite{samarakoon2022niddarxiv}, and later work reports a weighted $F_1$ score of up to 99.95\%~\cite{ilias2024moe5g}. Standardized NetFlow feature sets have made cross-corpus experiments feasible~\cite{sarhan2021netflow,sarhan2022standard}. The quality of benchmark corpora is, however, itself a source of error. Labeling and flow-construction faults have been documented in CIC-IDS2017~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting}, and design flaws that allow a classifier to score well without learning the attack have been found repeatedly across NIDS benchmarks~\cite{flood2024smells}.

\subsection{Evaluation Practice and Dataset Shift}

Intrusion detection results are often overly optimistic because of closed-world evaluation~\cite{sommer2010outside}, neglect of the base rate~\cite{axelsson2000baserate}, sampling bias and data leakage~\cite{arp2022dosdonts,kapoor2023leakage}, and temporal or spatial bias in the data split~\cite{pendlebury2019tesseract}. Apruzzese et al. examine the gap between reported and operational effectiveness across security domains~\cite{apruzzese2023role}, and Kus et al. observe a similar pattern in industrial intrusion detection when detectors are re-evaluated on data on which they were not tuned~\cite{kus2022falsesense}. The cross-dataset generalization of such detectors has repeatedly been found to be poor~\cite{dhooge2020interdataset,layeghy2022generalisability,layeghy2023benchmarking}. The work most closely related to ours is that of Abraheem and Edhirig, who transfer XGBoost between the same two corpora using 15 harmonized features and report zero-shot balanced accuracies per attack family of 0.50 to 0.56 from NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD to 5G-NIDD and of 0.52 to 0.83 in the reverse direction~\cite{abraheem2026bidirectional}. Our transfer results are consistent with theirs in both direction and magnitude. Relative to that study, the present paper contributes the protocol ladder with input-blind baselines, reference results measured within the target corpus, the evaluation of a published pipeline under the same ladder, an audit of the target labels, and the operational and latency criteria. We also draw on methods from the dataset-shift literature, namely label-shift estimation from unlabeled data~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,lipton2018bbse}, calibration and its behavior under shift~\cite{guo2017calibration,ovadia2019trust,fawcett2007pav}, shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing}, and covariance alignment for domain adaptation~\cite{sun2016coral}. For the comparison of classifiers under repeated resampling, we follow Nadeau and Bengio~\cite{nadeau2003inference}.

\section{Deployment Criteria and Threat Model}
\label{sec:criteria}

This section describes the system and threat model, defines the three quantities that the evaluation measures, and combines them into a deployability test.

\subsection{System and Threat Model}

A near-real-time RIC serves a set of E2 nodes (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}) to which user devices, including IoT devices, are attached. The adversary controls a subset of these devices and uses them to perform flooding, scanning, brute-force login, or web attacks, which are the attack categories present in our corpora. The adversary does not control the RIC, the E2 interface, or the xApp. The defender's xApp consumes either radio telemetry windows delivered through E2SM-KPM or flow records produced by a flow exporter co-located with the user plane, and it may act through E2SM-RC. Since E2 does not carry packets, a flow-level detector requires such an exporter outside the RIC. The cost and placement of this exporter are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc:practice}.

\subsection{Criterion 1: Generalization Across Deployments}

Let \DA{} and \DB{} denote two independently collected corpora represented in a shared feature space. A model $f_{\theta}$ trained on \DA{} is characterized by the difference between its held-out score on \DA{} and its score on \DB{}. Because the two corpora differ in attack prevalence (Table~\ref{tab:corpora}), a difference in macro-$F_1$ combines a change in the classifier's behavior with a change in class balance; indeed, even a classifier that ignores its input obtains a different macro-$F_1$ when the prevalence changes. We therefore report the difference in balanced accuracy, which is invariant to changes in prevalence,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{BA}} = \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{A}^{\mathrm{test}}\right) - \mathrm{BA}\!\left(f_{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{B}\right),
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}
together with the macro-$F_1$ gap $\Delta_{F_1}$ and its difference from the corresponding gap of the majority-class baseline. Covariate shift is quantified feature by feature as
\begin{equation}
\bar{W}_{1} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{F}} W_{1}\!\left(Q_{j}(P_{A}^{(j)}),\, Q_{j}(P_{B}^{(j)})\right),
\label{eq:shift}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{j}$ denotes the quantile transform of feature $j$, fitted on the source training data only. If each corpus were normalized by its own quantiles, both marginals would be mapped to the uniform distribution, and every term would be zero.

\subsection{Criterion 2: Alert Burden Under a Realistic Base Rate}

Let $\lambda_{b}$ denote the benign arrival rate in the detector's own unit, that is, flow records per hour for a flow-level detector and telemetry windows per hour for a window-level detector. A benign UE generates 225 windows of 16\,s per hour. The number of false alerts per hour is
\begin{equation}
A(\tau) = \lambda_{b}\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau),
\label{eq:alerts}
\end{equation}
and, for an operational attack prevalence $\pi$, the fraction of alerts that correspond to attacks is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PPV}(\tau) = \frac{\pi R(\tau)}{\pi R(\tau) + (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}(\tau)}.
\label{eq:ppv}
\end{equation}
Neither $\pi$ nor $\lambda_{b}$ can be measured from the corpora, and both are declared and varied over a range. Because $\mathrm{PPV}$ depends only on $\pi$ and $A$ scales linearly with $\lambda_{b}$, we also report the number of false alerts per $10^{6}$ benign flows, which is independent of $\lambda_b$.

\subsection{Criterion 3: Latency Against the Control Budget}

The decision latency of an xApp can be decomposed as
\begin{equation}
L = t_{\mathrm{ind}} + t_{\mathrm{feat}} + t_{\mathrm{inf}} + t_{\mathrm{act}} + t_{q},
\label{eq:latency}
\end{equation}
where the terms correspond to indication decoding, feature construction, inference, control encoding, and queueing. Only $t_{\mathrm{feat}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{inf}}$ are measured in this study, because the remaining three terms require a RIC in the measurement path. Conformance is a property of the tail of the latency distribution: for a budget $B$ and a tolerated violation rate $\varepsilon$, we require $q_{1-\varepsilon}(L) \le B$. Since the O-RAN E2 specification allows near-real-time latency requirements anywhere between 10\,ms and 1\,s~\cite{etsi_e2gap}, we report the smallest budget that is met rather than a verdict for a single chosen value.

\subsection{A Deployability Test}

The three criteria are combined into a test that a detector either passes or fails:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{P}(f_{\theta}) = {} & \ind\!\left[\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}} \le \delta\right] \cdot \ind\!\left[\overline{q}_{0.99}(L) \le B\right] \\
& \cdot \ind\!\big[\exists\,\tau:\ R(\tau)\ge r_{\min},\ A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \\
& \qquad\quad \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho\big],
\end{split}
\label{eq:deploy}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{\Delta}_{\mathrm{BA}}$ and $\overline{q}_{0.99}$ denote upper 95\% confidence bounds, so that a pass cannot depend on a favorable point estimate, and all alert conditions are evaluated on the target corpus. The minimum recall $r_{\min}$ prevents a detector that never raises an alert from satisfying the alert term. The operating threshold is chosen as the most sensitive admissible threshold,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\tau^{\star} = \arg\max_{\tau} R(\tau) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad & R(\tau) \ge r_{\min},\ A(\tau) \le A_{\max}, \\
& \mathrm{PPV}(\tau) \ge \rho .
\end{split}
\label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
We propose this test as a reporting instrument rather than as an O-RAN requirement. Its bounds represent operator requirements, and Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate} reports the values used in this study and the sensitivity of the verdict to them.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the corpora and the feature spaces, the detectors, the evaluation protocols, and the statistical analysis.

\subsection{Corpora}

\DA{} is NetsLab-5GORAN-IDD~\cite{abedzadeh2025netslab}, which was captured on a physical OpenAirInterface O-RAN testbed and contains benign traffic and five attack categories: denial of service (DoS), distributed denial of service (DDoS), probing, brute force, and web attacks. The corpus provides two layers that cannot be joined record by record from the published files, namely flow records exported by Zeek at the central unit and radio telemetry sampled at 1\,Hz at the distributed unit. Both layers are used in this study. The radio layer is aggregated into windows of 16 consecutive records of one UE, each summarized by the mean and standard deviation of 16 KPM fields. Its 30 capture sessions are recovered from gaps of more than five minutes in the timestamps. Fard et al. describe the same corpus as \SessRunN{} experiment runs~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}. A gap threshold of \SessRunGapMin{} to \SessRunGapMax{}\,s recovers exactly \SessRunN{} segments, each with a single attack category, and each of our sessions is a union of complete segments. The five-minute rule merges runs that were recorded consecutively (\SessMultiSub{} of the 30 sessions contain two or three attack subcategories), which ensures that such runs always fall on the same side of a split. Each recovered session carries the same category label in every record, so that a radio label is effectively a session label and every window of an attack session is an attack window. The sessions follow the capture schedule (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}), with nine benign sessions on days 0 to 6, the attack categories recorded in blocks on days 47 to 53, and one final benign session. \DB{} is 5G-NIDD~\cite{samarakoon2022nidd,siriwardhana2025descriptor}, which was captured on a 5G test network at a different institution and contains flow records exported by Argus with two attack categories, DoS and probing. Its published files contain no addresses, ports, or timestamps, but they retain the Argus record counter and file offset. Because the offset returns to its initial value at every file boundary, 20 capture files can be recovered. These correspond to two passes over the same ten captures, one per base station, and the published \texttt{Combined.csv} also restarts its row index at the beginning of the second pass. Each attack file contains benign traffic together with one attack type. A third corpus, \DC{}, serves as a second transfer target from a third site. It consists of the flow records of a 5G core testbed (Open5GS in Docker) published by Nugraha et al.~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}. Only three of its files contain flow records, each with one attack (a SYN flood, an ICMP flood, and a PFCP session-deletion attack), for a total of \ThNflows{} flows, of which \ThNattack{} are attacks. Its benign flows comprise user traffic and the signalling of the core network itself. \DC{} was exported by NFStream, a third flow exporter, which counts link-layer bytes and, for flows inside the GTP-U user-plane tunnel, the 44 bytes of encapsulation. We remove both overheads per packet so that the shared columns contain the IP bytes of the inner packet, as they do for \DA{}. Table~\ref{tab:corpora} summarizes the datasets.

\subsection{Shared Feature Space}

Because Zeek and Argus share no column names, the flow feature space is defined by the meaning of each field (Table~\ref{tab:features}). It comprises 15 concepts realized as 18 columns, namely seven counts reported by both exporters, a one-hot encoded protocol with a fixed list of levels, and seven derived quantities computed identically for both corpora. One mapping requires particular care. Zeek's \texttt{src\_bytes} counts application payload, which is zero for most floods and scans, whereas Argus's \texttt{SrcBytes} counts complete packets. The correct counterpart of \texttt{SrcBytes} is Zeek's \texttt{src\_ip\_bytes}, with 40 and 42 bytes per packet, respectively. A lower bound on duration and the transform $\log(1+x)$ for unbounded quantities are applied identically to both corpora, and scaling is fitted on the source training data only. No address, port, or timestamp is used as a feature.

Before any result was computed with it, we also defined an exporter-robust subset of nine columns, consisting of the protocol and the five per-packet means and direction ratios. The two exporters differ in where they terminate a flow, which affects duration, rates, and totals. Per-packet means and direction ratios are less affected, but they are not invariant. Splitting a flow changes them whenever its packets differ, for example when handshake and teardown packets are assigned to different records, and the two exporters count the same packet as 40 and 42 bytes, which corresponds to a fixed offset of about 5\% for minimum-size packets.

\subsection{Detectors}

Six supervised architectures and two trivial baselines are evaluated under every protocol. The supervised models are LR, a DT of depth 12, an RF~\cite{breiman2001random}, XGB~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, the HGB implementation of scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, and an MLP, and they are compared with a majority-class predictor and a stratified sampler of the training prior. Because the baselines ignore their input, any change in their score between two protocols reflects a change in class composition alone. All supervised models use the same policy for class imbalance, namely balanced class weights, which are applied as sample weights for the MLP and through \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} for XGB. The hyperparameters are fixed and were not tuned (Table~\ref{tab:hyper}). Tuning on held-out source data would favor structure that is specific to the source, which is precisely the effect that the transfer experiments measure, and tuning on the target would leak target information into training.

Three further model families are used to address specific questions. Two sequence models, a GRU~\cite{cho2014gru} and a one-dimensional convolutional network, operate on the raw 16-step KPM sequences of the same radio windows. Two unsupervised detectors, an isolation forest~\cite{liu2008isolation} and an autoencoder, are trained on benign source flows only, and their alarm threshold is fixed at the 99th percentile of the scores of benign source flows. Finally, a CORAL variant~\cite{sun2016coral} re-colors the source flows to the covariance of the unlabeled target before training. This is the only experiment in which a detector is exposed to target data.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}

Splits are group-disjoint wherever a group key is available, and an automated check rejects any split in which a group appears on both sides. On the radio layer, the group key is the capture session, and three protocols are compared: a random split of windows, a run-disjoint split, and a category-stratified run-disjoint split that keeps sessions of every attack category on both sides, so that no test category is absent from training. Two further designs address the effect of time (Section~\ref{sec:res:drift}). In the first, the latest or the earliest session of every category is held out and compared with draws that hold out a random session of every category. In the second, each benign session is held out individually. On the flow layer, the group key is the source address (318 groups). For each of 20 split seeds, a subsample of 300,000 flows is drawn from \DA{} and divided by source address. Every model is then evaluated on the whole of \DB{}, whereas the reverse direction trains on a random split of \DB{} and evaluates on \DA{}. Within \DB{} itself, the in-target references either hold out four of the 20 capture files or train on the pass of one base station and test on the pass of the other (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}). Apart from these references, CORAL, the domain classifier, and the prior estimates of Section~\ref{sec:res:calibration}, all of which are identified as such, no transform, threshold, or model choice uses \DB{}, and every access to it is logged.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Uncertainty is estimated over 20 split seeds. Because the splits resample a single population of 30 sessions or 318 source addresses, their training and test sets overlap, and a paired $t$-test over split means underestimates the variance. We therefore report Nadeau--Bengio corrected intervals~\cite{nadeau2003inference}, which scale the variance by $1/J + n_{\mathrm{test}}/n_{\mathrm{train}}$ for $J$ splits. Holm correction is applied within each family of tests, where a family consists of one metric and one comparison (a protocol pair or a transfer direction) across the six supervised architectures, and the two sequence models form a separate family. Operating points are computed from confusion counts pooled over folds, and Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts} explains why averaging per-fold precision is inappropriate. Pooled counts include a sample once for every seed in which it is tested, and samples within a cluster are correlated, so a binomial interval computed from them would be much too narrow. Intervals for pooled operating points are instead obtained from a cluster bootstrap with 2,000 replicates, which resamples with replacement both the split seeds and the clusters, that is, radio sessions, \DA{} source addresses, or \DB{} capture files.

A decision that exceeds the budget is still acted upon and recorded as a violation, since a late mitigation is generally preferable to none. \section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All measurements were performed on a single workstation with a 13th Gen Intel Core i9-13900H processor (14 cores, 20 threads), 47.6\,GB of memory, and Windows 11 Pro build 26120, using Python 3.11.9, scikit-learn 1.8.0, XGBoost 3.2.0, NumPy 2.3.5, and ONNX Runtime 1.30.0. The sequence models were trained with PyTorch 2.14 on the CPU in a separate environment. A complete list of packages is provided with the results. No RIC was involved in any experiment. The detector was not deployed as an xApp, no E2 agent was run, no component was containerized, and all latency figures are therefore emulated.

The comparison of protocols on the radio layer uses 20 split seeds with two model seeds each, and the remaining radio-layer and flow-layer experiments use 20 split seeds with one model seed. Calibration uses 10 split seeds of a three-way source split into training, calibration, and test groups. The declared operational parameters are an attack prevalence of $\pi = 0.002$ and a benign arrival rate of $\lambda_{b} = 240{,}000$ flows per hour, and both are varied over a range. For the radio layer, false alerts are reported per benign UE-hour. Neither parameter was obtained from an operator. The chosen rate of about 67 flows per second is below the several hundred flows per second that Jin et al. expect at a heavily loaded base station of a large LTE network~\cite{jin2013softcell}, so the reported alert volumes are likely to underestimate those of an edge site serving several cells. Latency is measured per call with a monotonic clock, with garbage collection enabled, after 1,000 warm-up calls, over 20,000 calls, and with one thread per library. Distribution-free confidence intervals are reported for every quantile.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The results are presented in the order of the evaluation ladder. We first examine protocol sensitivity within one corpus, followed by the effects of time order and benign sessions, the properties of the target corpus and of a published result on it, transfer across corpora and the composition of the transfer gap, and a third corpus. We then report the alert burden, calibration, and latency.

\subsection{Evaluation-Protocol Sensitivity}
\label{sec:res:protocol}

Table~\ref{tab:leakage} and Fig.~\ref{fig:leakage} compare three protocols on the radio layer. Relative to a run-disjoint split, a random split raises macro-$F_1$ by \LeakMin{} to \LeakMax{} across the six architectures, or by \LeakAvg{} on average, and the gain is positive in at least \LeakSeedsMin{} of the 20 splits for every architecture. The trivial baselines, in contrast, show no gain. The corrected interval for the average gain is [\LeakAvgNbLo, \LeakAvgNbHi] ($p = \LeakAvgPnb$). With only 30 capture sessions, the magnitude of the effect cannot be estimated precisely, and the gain of no individual architecture remains significant after Holm correction. For comparison, an uncorrected paired $t$-test would yield $p = \LeakAvgPt$ for the same data.

The gain cannot be attributed to test categories that are absent from training, since every test category also appeared in training in all 20 run-disjoint splits. The category-stratified protocol guarantees this property by construction and yields a gain of \LeakStratMin{} to \LeakStratMax{}. However, this protocol moves sessions of rare categories into the test set, which makes its test sets more difficult, and we do not interpret its larger gain as additional evidence. A model-free probe helps to identify the underlying mechanism. Under a random split, \NNSame\% of the test windows have their nearest training window in the same capture session (\NNSameMin\% to \NNSameMax\% across splits), and a one-nearest-neighbor (1-NN) lookup achieves a macro-$F_1$ of \NNoneRandom{}, compared with \NNoneRun{} under a run-disjoint split (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). Because every radio label is also a session label, recognizing the session is sufficient to predict the label, and a random split allows any model, including a lookup table, to exploit this. Under the run-disjoint split, the lookup outperforms LR and DT and comes within \NNgapBest{} of the best supervised architecture, which suggests that, on this layer, the learned models add little beyond the retrieval of similar windows. The two sequence models in Table~\ref{tab:sequence}, which operate on the raw 16-step KPM sequences instead of window summaries, exhibit the same pattern more clearly. They achieve \SeqRandomMin{} to \SeqRandomMax{} under a random split and \SeqRDmin{} to \SeqRDmax{} under a run-disjoint split, a gain of \SeqGainMin{} to \SeqGainMax{} whose corrected intervals exclude zero for both models (Holm-adjusted $p \le \SeqPholmMax{}$ within the pair).

\subsection{Time Order and Benign Sessions}
\label{sec:res:drift}

Although the radio layer spans \DriftDays{} days, its capture order follows the scenario schedule (Table~\ref{tab:benign}). Nine benign sessions were recorded on days 0 to 6, the five attack categories were recorded in blocks on days 47 to 53, and a final benign session was recorded on day 53. Because every radio label is a session label, each session is either benign or belongs to a single attack category throughout. A split based on session order consequently holds out entire attack categories. For training fractions between 0.4 and 0.7, the latest sessions contain \DvAuditUnseenMin{} to \DvAuditUnseenMax{} attack categories that are absent from training, together with a single benign session. Such a split measures the detection of unseen categories rather than temporal drift, and we therefore do not report it as a drift estimate.

To keep category coverage fixed, we held out the latest session of every category, including the benign category, and compared the result with \DvNctrl{} control draws, each of which holds out a randomly chosen session of every category (Table~\ref{tab:timesplit}). When the latest sessions are held out, balanced accuracy ranges from \DvFwdBAmin{} to \DvFwdBAmax{}, compared with control means of \DvCtrlBAmin{} to \DvCtrlBAmax{}, and yet \DvFwdPctMin\% to \DvFwdPctMax\% of the control draws score equally low. When the earliest sessions are held out, balanced accuracy ranges from \DvRevBAmin{} to \DvRevBAmax{}, a level that \DvRevPctMin\% to \DvRevPctMax\% of the control draws match or exceed. The effect of time order thus remains within the ordinary variation between sessions.

The variation is determined largely by which benign session is placed in the test set. When the benign sessions are held out one at a time (Table~\ref{tab:benign} and Fig.~\ref{fig:benign}), \BenLowN{} of the ten sessions yield a false positive rate of at most \BenLowMax{}, whereas sessions \BenHighSessions{} (days \BenHighDays{}) yield rates between \BenHighMin{} and \BenHighMax{}. The final benign session, the only one recorded during the attack period, yields a false positive rate of \BenLateFPR{}, which corresponds to \BenLateFA{} false alerts per benign UE-hour, but two sessions from the first week yield comparable rates. With only ten benign sessions, a change over time cannot be separated from this heterogeneity. In practical terms, the false positive rate of a detector at a new site cannot be inferred from its rate on the benign sessions on which it was tested.

\subsection{The Target Corpus: Labels and a Published Result}
\label{sec:res:target}

\emph{Label conflicts.} Before evaluating transfer to \DB{}, we examined whether its labels are a function of its flow records, and found that this is not the case for half of the corpus. Of the \NDbFlows{} flows, \LcShareAll\% (\LcFlowsAll) share every field except the row index, \texttt{Seq}, and \texttt{Offset} with a flow that carries the opposite label (Table~\ref{tab:conflict}, Fig.~\ref{fig:conflict}). All of these flows lie in the two UDP-flood captures, where they account for \LcBenignFileConf\% of file~\LcBenignFile{} and \LcAttackFileConf\% of file~\LcAttackFile{}, one file from each base station. The pattern is highly specific. Each of the \LcTwinAttack{} flows labeled as UDP flood in file~\LcAttackFile{} has an identical copy labeled as benign in file~\LcBenignFile{}, and the counts are equal for \LcTwinEqual{} of the \LcTwinRecords{} distinct records. The same traffic appears to have been captured at both stations and labeled differently at each. These copies constitute \LcBenignTwinPct\% of the benign class of the corpus. Because the published files do not indicate which label is correct, every \DB{} result below is reported both with and without these benign copies, and no label is modified.

These conflicts impose an upper bound on the performance of any classifier that uses the record content. Even when evaluated on the flows from which it was constructed, the best possible function of the \LcNFeat{} content fields achieves a balanced accuracy of \LcCeilNative{}. In the 18 shared columns the corresponding bound is \LcCeilShared{}, which is the level reached by DT, XGB, HGB, and the MLP when they are trained and tested on a random split of \DB{} (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}). When \texttt{Seq} and \texttt{Offset} are added, every record becomes unique and the bound rises to \LcCeilPos{}.

\emph{References within the target.} To distinguish the cost of transfer from the limits imposed by \DB{} itself, we also trained and tested the six architectures on \DB{} alone (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}). On a random split, they reach \TgtRandShBAmin{} to \TgtRandShBAmax{} in the shared space, which corresponds to the bound given above, and \TgtRandShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtRandShCleanBAmax{} when the benign copies are excluded from the test set. On held-out capture files, they reach \TgtFileShBAmin{} to \TgtFileShBAmax{} in the shared space and \TgtFileNatBAmin{} to \TgtFileNatBAmax{} with native features, although none of the three draws held out a UDP-flood capture. Across the two base stations, within a single corpus and with a single exporter, balanced accuracy decreases to \TgtSiteABShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteABShBAmax{} from station 1 to station 2 and to \TgtSiteBAShBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShBAmax{} in the opposite direction. Both decreases are caused by the copies. A model trained on station 1, where the flood copies are labeled benign, misses the floods of station 2, whereas a model trained on station 2 flags the benign-labeled copies of station 1 and scores \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmin{} to \TgtSiteBAShCleanBAmax{} when these copies are removed from the test set. On flows whose labels are determined by the record content, the shared space separates the classes of \DB{} almost perfectly, which indicates that the target corpus itself is not the factor that limits transfer.

\subsection{Transfer Across Corpora}
\label{sec:res:transfer}

Table~\ref{tab:transfer} and Fig.~\ref{fig:transfer} report the results of transfer from \DA{} to \DB{}. In terms of macro-$F_1$, every supervised model loses \TrFoneDropMin{} to \TrFoneDropMax{}. However, the majority baseline, which uses no features, also loses \TrMajDrop{}, because \DB{} has a lower attack prevalence than \DA{}. Relative to this baseline, the loss in macro-$F_1$ lies between \TrDiDmin{} and \TrDiDmax{}, and none of the macro-$F_1$ losses remains significant after correction. Macro-$F_1$ is therefore not an appropriate measure for this comparison. Balanced accuracy, which is unaffected by prevalence, shows the loss clearly. It decreases from \TrSrcBAmin{}--\TrSrcBAmax{} on held-out source data to \TrTgtBAmin{}--\TrTgtBAmax{} on \DB{}, a drop of \TrBAdropMin{} to \TrBAdropMax{} that is significant for \TrSigBA{} of the six architectures after correction, is positive in at least \TrBAseedsMin{} of the 20 splits for every architecture, and averages \TrBAdropAvg{} [\TrBAdropAvgNbLo, \TrBAdropAvgNbHi]. The trivial baselines remain unchanged, and the target ROC-AUC lies between \TrTgtAUCmin{} and \TrTgtAUCmax{}.

The detectors remain above chance on the target, although only moderately so. The average target balanced accuracy is \TrTgtBAavg{}, and the corrected interval excludes 0.5 for \TrChanceSig{} of the six architectures (\TrChanceSigHolm{} after Holm correction). For reference, the stratified sampler achieves a macro-$F_1$ of \TrStratFloor{} on \DB{}, and a fair coin would achieve \TrFairCoin{}. Both values are reported because the value of a macro-$F_1$ floor depends on the prior from which it samples. According to every prevalence-independent measure, LR transfers best (balanced accuracy \TrLRtgtBA{}, ROC-AUC \TrLRtgtAUC{}), whereas the MLP, which achieves the highest held-out source balanced accuracy (\TrMLPsrcBA{}), reaches \TrMLPtgtBA{} on the target. Source performance thus does not predict target performance in a prevalence-independent metric, as the rank correlation across the six architectures is \RhoBA{} in balanced accuracy ($p=\RhoBAP$). In macro-$F_1$ the rank correlation is \RhoFone{} ($p=\RhoFoneP$), but rankings in macro-$F_1$ change with prevalence, and six architectures provide little precision for either estimate.

\emph{Effect of the target labels.} Part of this loss is attributable to the label conflict described in Section~\ref{sec:res:target}. Detectors that learned to recognize a UDP flood on \DA{} detect the floods in capture file~\LcAttackFile{} and, by the same rule, flag between \NetCopyFlagMin{} and \NetCopyFlagMax{} of the benign-labeled copies in file~\LcBenignFile{}, whereas LR detects neither. When the copies are excluded, in a deterministic re-run of 10 of the 20 split seeds, the target balanced accuracy is \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}, namely \TrCleanOthMin{} to \TrCleanOthMax{} for the five nonlinear architectures and \TrCleanLR{} for LR. The loss relative to held-out source data decreases to \TrCleanDropMin{} to \TrCleanDropMax{} and is significant for \TrCleanSig{} of the six architectures after correction. The conflicts thus reverse the apparent ranking of the detectors. On all flows LR appears to transfer best, whereas on the flows whose labels are determined by the record content it performs worst. Compared with the in-target reference on these flows (Table~\ref{tab:target_ref}), transfer still falls short by \TrCleanShortMin{} to \TrCleanShortMax{} in balanced accuracy.

\subsection{Composition of the Transfer Gap}
\label{sec:res:anatomy}

\emph{Separability.} A classifier trained to distinguish \DA{} flows from \DB{} flows achieves a balanced accuracy of \DomShared{} on the 18 shared columns and of \DomRobust{} on the nine exporter-robust columns alone. The largest per-feature shifts concern the protocol mix (most \DA{} flows are TCP, whereas most \DB{} flows are UDP), the direction ratios, and the packet counts (Fig.~\ref{fig:shift}). Moreover, the robust columns shift as much as the segmentation-dependent columns ($\bar{W}_1$ of \WoneRobust{} compared with \WoneNonrobust). The corpora thus differ in traffic composition, which no choice of exporter would remove, as well as in flow segmentation, which a common exporter would remove.

\emph{Model families and adaptation.} Table~\ref{tab:arms} evaluates the same splits under three modifications, none of which yields a transferable detector. Restricting the detectors to the nine exporter-robust columns raises the held-out source balanced accuracy to \RobSrcBAmin{} to \RobSrcBAmax{}, but the target balanced accuracy falls to \RobBAmin{} to \RobBAmax{}, which is no better than chance. This result indicates that the columns that do not depend on flow segmentation carry the shift in traffic composition described above. CORAL, which uses unlabeled target features, yields a target balanced accuracy of \CoralBAmin{} to \CoralBAmax{}. Only the MLP benefits from it (\CoralMLPtgtBA{} compared with \TrMLPtgtBA{} without adaptation), and the target false positive rate increases to \CoralFPRmin{} to \CoralFPRmax{}. The novelty detectors trained on benign traffic alone already fail on held-out source data, because floods and scans resemble short benign flows in this feature space.

\emph{Cost of the shared space.} Projecting \DA{} onto the 18 shared columns reduces the in-distribution balanced accuracy by \HarmBAmin{} to \HarmBAmax{} relative to the use of all \NFullFeat{} non-port Zeek features (Table~\ref{tab:harm}). \subsection{A Third Corpus}

To examine whether these results depend on the particular pair of corpora, we added \DC{}, the flow records of a 5G core testbed~\cite{nugraha2025fivegdatasets}, as a second target. The main source of error is the benign traffic of the new site. \subsection{Alert Burden}
\label{sec:res:alerts}

Table~\ref{tab:pooled} reports pooled operating points at $\tau=0.5$ for three evaluation sets, together with 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals. On the radio layer, the six detectors have a pooled window-level false positive rate of \RadFPRmin{} to \RadFPRmax{}, which corresponds to \RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} false alerts per benign UE-hour, and, at $\pi=0.002$, an operational precision of \RadPPVmin{} to \RadPPVmax{}, compared with a corpus precision of \RadPrecMin{} to \RadPrecMax{}. These point estimates are not stable. The false positive rate depends on which of the ten benign sessions are included in the test set (Table~\ref{tab:benign}), and the bootstrap interval of the pooled rate spans \RadCbFPRlo{} to \RadCbFPRhi{} across architectures, or \RadCbFAlo{} to \RadCbFAhi{} false alerts per benign UE-hour. Likewise, the interval for operational precision at $\tau=0.5$ spans \RadCbPPVlo{} to \RadCbPPVhi{}. Thirty sessions are insufficient to determine the alert burden of the radio layer, and they show only that it depends on the benign conditions that a detector encounters. On held-out \DA{} flows, the false positive rate is \NetSrcFPRmin{} to \NetSrcFPRmax{}, which at $\lambda_b = 240{,}000$ benign flows per hour corresponds to \NetSrcFAmin{} to \NetSrcFAmax{} false alerts per hour. On \DB{}, the false positive rate is \NetTgtFPRmin{} to \NetTgtFPRmax{}, or \NetTgtFAmin{} to \NetTgtFAmax{} false alerts per hour. Without the benign copies described in Section~\ref{sec:res:target}, it is \NetCleanFPRmin{} to \NetCleanFPRmax{}, which still amounts to \NetCleanFAmin{} to \NetCleanFAmax{} false alerts per hour, and the operational precision is \NetCleanPPVmin{} to \NetCleanPPVmax{}.

Increasing the decision threshold improves precision only on held-out \DA{} flows (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}). Because the best threshold is selected on the evaluation data, the following values should be regarded as upper bounds. With recall kept above 10\%, high thresholds reach an operational precision of \NetSrcPPVbest{} on held-out \DA{} flows, whereas the best value achieved by any detector on \DB{} is \NetTgtPPVbest{}, or \NetCleanPPVbest{} without the copies. On the radio layer, the best pooled value is \RadPPVbest{}, but its bootstrap interval reaches 1.0 for \RadCbBestHiN{} of the six architectures. This occurs because a resample that omits the benign sessions that are flagged almost entirely leaves thresholds at which no false positive occurs. The relation between corpus precision and operational precision is determined arithmetically by $\pi$. Varying $\pi$ from $10^{-4}$ to 0.5 changes the ratio between the two from \RadCollapseLowPi{} to \RadCollapseHighPi{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:prevalence}), so an operational precision is meaningful only when the assumed prevalence is stated.
\emph{Estimation of operational precision.} PPV is strongly nonlinear in the false positive rate near zero, so a fold that happens to contain no false positive yields $\mathrm{PPV}=1$ regardless of its recall. Averaging PPV over folds consequently favors the architecture that happens to draw the largest number of such folds. On the radio layer, the same counts yield a ratio between the best and the worst architecture of \EstPooled{} when pooled, \EstMedian{} as a median over folds, and \EstMean{} as a mean over folds (Table~\ref{tab:estimator}). Pooled counts are therefore used throughout this paper.

\subsection{Calibration and Prior Shift}
\label{sec:res:calibration}

Under transfer, the scores are poorly calibrated (Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}). We fitted three calibrators on a group-disjoint calibration fold drawn from the source (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). Whether a calibrator can alter the achievable operating points depends on whether it preserves the order of the scores. Temperature scaling preserves this order and changes ROC-AUC by at most \CalTempMax{}. Platt scaling is monotone, but its fitted slope can be negative, and for DT it inverted the ranking in \CalPlattFlipSeeds{} of \CalSeeds{} seeds, changing ROC-AUC by \CalPlattFlip. In that seed, the scores of the tree carried no ranking information on the calibration fold (ROC-AUC \PlattCalAUC{} on \PlattCalGroups{} held-out source addresses), although they ranked the test fold well (\PlattTestAUC). Platt scaling fitted the calibration fold correctly with a slope of \PlattSlope{} and, as a result, inverted the ranking on the test fold. This failure is attributable to small group-disjoint calibration folds, which need not resemble the test fold, rather than to Platt scaling itself. Isotonic regression is monotone but piecewise constant, so it merges scores into ties and, on its fitting data, yields the ROC convex hull~\cite{fawcett2007pav}. Its largest change in ROC-AUC ranged from \CalIsoMin{} to \CalIsoMax{} across architectures. None of the calibrators raises the target macro-$F_1$ above that of the raw scores for any architecture. Because they are fitted on a source containing \PrevDa\% attacks, they shift the scores toward that prior and flag more benign traffic on \DB{}.

A standard remedy for prior shift is to rescale the scores to the target prior~\cite{saerens2002adjusting}, which is, however, unknown in a deployment. Two estimators recover this prior from unlabeled data under the assumption that only the class balance changes, namely expectation maximization~\cite{saerens2002adjusting} and black-box shift estimation~\cite{lipton2018bbse}. In the present setting, this assumption does not hold. For a true target prior of \PriorTrue{}, the mean estimates per architecture range from \PriorMeanMin{} to \PriorMeanMax{}, and \PriorFracExtreme\% of the individual estimates fall below 0.05 or above 0.95. Moreover, rescaling with the true prior, which is an oracle setting, does not outperform the raw scores (Table~\ref{tab:calibration}). The limiting factor is therefore the discriminability of the detectors on the target, with ROC-AUC of \TrTgtAUCmin{} to \TrTgtAUCmax{}, rather than their calibration.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section discusses the implications of the results for O-RAN practice, applies the deployability test, and relates the results to previously reported findings.

\subsection{Implications for O-RAN Practice}
\label{sec:disc:practice}

\emph{Data path.} E2SM-KPM delivers aggregated measurements for each reporting period and does not carry packets. A window-level detector can operate inside the RIC on KPM reports, whereas a flow-level detector requires a flow exporter located near the user plane, either in the central unit or at the user-plane function, as well as a means of transporting its records to the xApp, for example through a custom E2 service model or the non-real-time data collection path. This transport contributes to $t_{\mathrm{ind}}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:latency}, which was not measured in this study.

\emph{Detection delay.} Computational latency accounts for only a small part of the total time to detection. A window-level decision cannot be made before the end of its window (16\,s in this study), and a flow-level decision cannot be made before the flow record is exported, which, for a long connection, requires an idle or active timeout to expire. The KPM reporting period and the flow timeouts should be reported together with the computational latency.

\emph{Acting on alerts.} An alert that triggers an E2SM-RC action, such as isolating or steering a UE, acts on a false alert in the same way as on a true one. At the false positive rates reported in Table~\ref{tab:pooled}, automatic mitigation on the radio layer would act against each benign UE \RadFAmin{} to \RadFAmax{} times per hour. Alerts of this quality may inform an operator or a non-real-time policy through A1, but they should not trigger automatic control actions.

\emph{Admission and monitoring.} The deployability test of Eq.~\eqref{eq:deploy} can serve as an admission check before an xApp is enabled at a new site, provided that labeled benign and attack traffic is available from that site. After admission, unsupervised shift detection~\cite{rabanser2019failing} can indicate when the incoming traffic departs from the training distribution. Because the corpora studied here can be distinguished with a balanced accuracy of \DomShared{}, such a monitor would raise an alarm immediately.

\subsection{Relation to Reported Results}

Most published results for O-RAN and 5G intrusion detection are in-distribution scores obtained on corpora that differ from ours, and our own in-distribution scores under a random split are similarly high (Table~\ref{tab:leakage}). \section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{Flow exporters.} \DA{}, \DB{}, and \DC{} were produced by three different tools (Zeek, Argus, and NFStream), and it was not possible to re-extract \DA{} or \DB{} with a common exporter, because our copy of \DB{} contains Argus flow records only and no Zeek installation was available on the measurement host. The domain classifier and the robust subset show that the corpora differ in traffic composition as well as in flow segmentation, but they do not quantify how much of the transfer gap a common exporter would remove. The base-station split within \DB{} (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}) changes the site while keeping the exporter fixed, and it therefore addresses part of this question.

\emph{Target labels.} Of the benign flows in \DB{}, \LcBenignTwinPct\% are exact copies of records that are labeled as UDP flood elsewhere in the corpus (Section~\ref{sec:res:target}). Because the published files do not indicate which label is correct, every operating point on \DB{} is reported both with and without these flows. In addition, the assignment of the two capture passes to the two base stations is inferred from the repeated capture schedule and the restarted row index, since it is not stated in the dataset documentation.

\emph{Radio capture schedule.} Nine of the ten benign radio sessions were recorded six weeks before the first attack session, mostly with a single UE, and every radio label is a session label. A radio detector may learn to separate capture periods in addition to attacks. The only benign session recorded during the attack period is flagged almost entirely, but the same holds for two early sessions (Table~\ref{tab:benign}), and ten benign sessions are not sufficient to distinguish between the two explanations.

\emph{Number of corpora.} Three corpora are sufficient to establish that the transfer gap recurs, but not to characterize how it varies across deployments. The third corpus originates from a 5G core testbed rather than from an O-RAN deployment, contains only flood attacks and a control-plane attack, and comprises three capture files, which are too few for cluster-based intervals. No further O-RAN corpus with paired radio telemetry was available to us.

\emph{Latency.} All latency figures were obtained by emulation on a single Windows host without CPU isolation, and $t_{\mathrm{ind}}$, $t_{\mathrm{act}}$, and $t_{q}$ were not measured. CPU and memory utilization under load were not measured either.

\emph{Operational parameters and the deployability test.} The prevalence $\pi = 0.002$ and the benign arrival rate $\lambda_b$ are declared values and were not measured on an operational network. Both are varied over a range, and operational precision depends on $\pi$ alone. The bounds of the deployability test were chosen by us (Section~\ref{sec:disc:predicate}). Although Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} reports the verdict under other bounds, an operator may weigh recall, precision, and alert volume differently, and the test does not account for detection delay or for the cost of a missed attack.

\emph{Sample size.} The radio layer comprises 30 capture sessions, each of which contains a single scenario. Session and attack scenario are therefore confounded, and the protocol and temporal analyses can detect only large effects. Because only ten benign sessions are available, the false positive rate of the radio layer, and hence its operational precision, carries a wide interval (Section~\ref{sec:res:alerts}). The transfer statistics vary the training set over 20 seeds but evaluate a single fixed target corpus, so they do not describe the variability across other deployments.

\emph{Scope.} This study evaluates binary detection only. Multi-class attribution, feature attribution~\cite{lundberg2017shap}, and the fusion of radio and flow features are left for future work. The published summary files cannot be joined record by record, so fusion requires the raw archives, as in~\cite{fard2026crosslayer}. Furthermore, three attack categories of \DA{} are absent from \DB{} and can therefore be tested in one direction only.

\section{Conclusion and Reporting Requirements}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study shows that a held-out score on a single corpus provides limited information about the behavior of a detection xApp in a different deployment. On the corpora examined here, a random split inflates the score, and a model-free lookup achieves a comparable gain, which is consistent with the recognition of capture sessions. The capture schedule of the radio corpus does not permit an estimate of temporal drift, and the false positive rate depends largely on the benign session that a detector encounters. A published accuracy of 99.9\% on the target corpus depends on record-position fields, and half of that corpus carries labels that cannot be determined from the record content. On flows with consistent labels, detectors transferred from the source corpus achieve a balanced accuracy of \TrCleanTgtBAmin{} to \TrCleanTgtBAmax{}, which is below the level attainable within the target corpus. On a third corpus, the detectors identify the SYN flood but flag a large share of the benign traffic. At a realistic base rate, no threshold yields a usable operational precision, and latency, the only criterion that is satisfied, was measured off-platform. We therefore propose that studies claiming a deployable O-RAN detection capability report at least the following: (i) performance on a corpus collected independently of the training data, in a prevalence-invariant metric such as balanced accuracy or ROC-AUC, beside input-blind baselines and a reference trained inside that corpus; (ii) the split protocol and its group key, results under a group-disjoint split, and the category coverage of any time-based split; (iii) the false positive rate on benign traffic, with its unit, the resulting alert rate, and the operational precision at a stated base rate, all from pooled confusion counts with intervals that resample clusters; (iv) the operating threshold, how it was chosen, and the recall it gives up; (v) intervals that account for overlap between resampled splits; (vi) tail latency with its stages, implementation, and host, measured on a real RIC before any conformance claim is made; and (vii) evidence that the labels are a function of the features used, with no identical records under opposite labels, and that no record-position or identifier field is a feature.
