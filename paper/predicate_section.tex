We instantiate Eq.~\eqref{eq:deploy} with the bounds below. No O-RAN specification defines them, so we chose them ourselves, and an operator would set them from its own traffic and staffing. The generalization bound is $\delta = 0.10$ in balanced accuracy, applied to the upper end of the corrected 95\% interval. The alert term asks for a threshold that detects at least half of the attacks ($r_{\min} = 0.5$), at which at least one alert in ten is real ($\rho = 0.1$ at $\pi = 0.002$), and which raises no more than $A_{\max} = 200$ false alerts per hour at $\lambda_{b} = 240{,}000$ benign flows per hour. All alert conditions are checked on \DB{}, since a detector is deployed on the target and not on the source. For latency we take the strictest budget the specification allows, $B = 10$\,ms, and evaluate it on the FlexRIC loop at a 10\,ms reporting period. There the upper bound of the 99th percentile is at most \RicLoopTenPnnHiMax{}\,ms for every architecture (Section~\ref{sec:res:latency}). On one host and with an emulated E2 node, the latency term holds for any budget in the specified range.

Table~\ref{tab:predicate} gives the verdict: no architecture passes (\PredPass{} of six). All of them fail the generalization term, with upper bounds of \PredGenMin{} to \PredGenMax{}, and all fail the alert term by a wide margin. At a recall of at least 0.5, the best operational precision on \DB{} is \PredPPVmax{}, and the fewest false alerts per hour are \PredFAmin{}. The verdict is robust to the choice of bounds. It holds for any $\rho$ above \PredPPVmax{}, any $A_{\max}$ below \PredFAmin{} per hour, and any $\delta$ below \PredGenMin{}, and the latency term plays no part in it. Neither does the declared prevalence. The false-alert count does not involve $\pi$ and exceeds $A_{\max}$ at every threshold, while the precision condition on its own would need $\pi \ge \PredPiStar$, \PredPiStarRatio{} times the declared value. The label conflicts of Section~\ref{sec:res:target} do not change the verdict either. On \DB{} without the benign copies, \PredCleanPass{} of six architectures pass, the best operational precision at a recall of at least 0.5 is \PredCleanPPVmax{}, and the fewest false alerts per hour are \PredCleanFAmin. The precision condition alone would hold there from $\pi = \PredCleanPiStar$, but the false-alert count still rules out every architecture.

How far would the bounds have to move? Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity} shows it. An architecture meets the alert term only if an operator accepts several thousand false alerts per hour at a precision of a few percent, and it meets the generalization term only if $\delta$ exceeds \PredGenMin{}. Latency would matter only once the first two terms were met, and in our loop it clears the smallest specified budget by about a factor of two.

\emph{Attainability of the test.} A test that nobody passes may just be too strict. The published 5G-NIDD pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:res:target} suggests otherwise. On its own random split, with the alert term evaluated on that split, \PubRzPassN{} of its five classifiers pass, reaching an operational precision of up to \PubRzPPVmax{} at $\pi = 0.002$ with as few as \PubRzFAmin{} false alerts per hour at $\lambda_b = 240{,}000$. The same pipeline fails one rung later, once the two position counters are removed, and at every rung after that, on all flows and on the flows without the benign copies alike (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The test can only be as informative as the evaluation it is applied to. On a random split that still contains record positions it merely confirms that split, and the pass vanishes as soon as the evaluation moves one step closer to deployment. Under the evaluations closest to deployment that the available corpora allow, the detectors in this paper fail.
