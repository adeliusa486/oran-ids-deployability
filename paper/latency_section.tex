Table~\ref{tab:latency} gives per-call latency on one host for three implementations of each model: scikit-learn with a one-row pandas DataFrame, as our experiment code calls it; scikit-learn with a NumPy array on one thread; and ONNX Runtime on one thread, whose \LatOnnxN{} exported models reproduce the decisions of scikit-learn exactly (decision agreement \LatOnnxAgreeMin). A call that does nothing has a 99th percentile of \LatFloorPnn{}\,ms, too little to distort the comparisons.

Implementation matters more than architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}). For LR, the DataFrame path takes \LatLRdfPfifty{}\,ms at the median against \LatLRarrPfifty{}\,ms for an array, a difference we attribute to input validation without having measured it. RF takes \LatRFarrPfifty{}\,ms on one thread and \LatRFonnxPfifty{}\,ms in ONNX Runtime, and \LatRFthreadsPfifty{}\,ms through its default thread pool, a configuration not in Table~\ref{tab:latency}. On the radio layer the 99th percentile under ONNX Runtime lies between \LatOnnxPnnMin{} and \LatOnnxPnnMax{}\,ms for all architectures, while scikit-learn with arrays reaches \LatArrPnnMax{}\,ms. Ranking architectures by latency mostly ranks implementations, so latencies from other hosts or languages cannot rank them. For context only, Obiuwevwi et al. report microsecond inference for logistic regression and a small MLP inside an OpenAirInterface and FlexRIC near-real-time RIC~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime}, within an order of magnitude of our ONNX Runtime figures.

Aggregating one 16-record window takes \LatAggPnn{}\,ms at the 99th percentile, and the upper bound of the 99th percentile of aggregation plus ONNX inference is at most \LatEtoEPnnHiMax{}\,ms for every architecture, within any budget in the specified range. A repeat that recorded every call for Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} gave medians of \LatRepMedRatioMin{} to \LatRepMedRatioMax{} times those in Table~\ref{tab:latency} (median ratio \LatRepMedRatio), as without CPU isolation latency depends on the state of the host, but that bound stayed at or below \LatRepEtoEPnnHiMax\,ms.

Table~\ref{tab:ric} adds the remaining terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:latency} for window-level detectors in the FlexRIC loop of Section~\ref{sec:setup}. The six models ran with a report every 10\,ms (\RicNten{} reports each after 200 warm-up reports), LR and RF also every 1\,ms (\RicNone{} after 1,000 warm-up reports), with \RicUEs{} UEs per report on average, all scored. At the 10\,ms period, delivery from the E2 node to the xApp takes \RicIndTenPfifty{}\,ms at the median and the control round trip \RicActTenPfifty{}\,ms. Inference in the loop needs at most \RicInfTenPnnMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile, several times the standalone figures; the loop uses the C API under WSL2 rather than the Python API on Windows, and we did not isolate which difference is responsible. Delivery and control account for at least \RicTransportShareMin\% of the mean loop time for every model, so once a RIC is in the path the model barely matters for latency. The full loop, from report to acknowledged control message, takes \RicLoopTenPnnMin{} to \RicLoopTenPnnMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile (upper 95\% bound at most \RicLoopTenPnnHiMax{}\,ms) and at most \RicLoopTenPnnnMax{}\,ms at the 99.9th, all within the smallest specified budget of 10\,ms on this emulated loop. The largest single value at this period was \RicLoopTenMax{}\,ms.

The loop ran faster at the 1\,ms period: its 99th percentile fell to \RicLoopOnePnnMin{} to \RicLoopOnePnnMax{}\,ms, with at most \RicQueueMax{} queued reports, no dropped report, and every term faster, inference included (at most \RicInfOnePnnMax{}\,ms). We did not control CPU power management, and a host that idles between sparse reports and pays to wake up would produce this pattern.

For a flow-level detector the expensive part is feature construction, not inference. On the three DDoS captures of \DA{}, our vectorized exporter (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) produces flow records at \LatExtrMin{} to \LatExtrMax{}\,ms per flow, \LatExtrSpeedMin{} to \LatExtrSpeedMax{} times faster than our per-packet reference implementation (\LatExtrRefMin{} to \LatExtrRefMax{}\,ms per flow), at a cost that grows with the packets per flow. On the \LatEqCaptures{} smaller captures, where re-running the reference is affordable, both agree exactly in packets and bytes on all \LatEqKeys{} shared IPv4 flow keys (the reference also emits \LatRefOnlyMin{} and \LatRefOnlyMax{} IPv6 link-local keys, which the IPv4-only vectorized exporter skips). Mapping a record onto the shared space with our pandas code adds \LatMapPfifty{}\,ms at the median, against at most \LatNetOnnxPnnMax{}\,ms for ONNX inference at the 99th percentile. Export and mapping alone thus take close to 10\,ms before any transport to the xApp, which our loop lacks, so we have no measured latency term for flow-level detectors and cannot say what a native user-plane exporter would cost.
