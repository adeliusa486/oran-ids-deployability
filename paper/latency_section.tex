Table~\ref{tab:latency} gives per-call latency on one host for three implementations of each model. The first calls scikit-learn with a one-row pandas DataFrame, as our experiment code does. The second calls scikit-learn with a NumPy array on one thread. The third runs ONNX Runtime on one thread and is used only where the exported model reproduces the decisions of scikit-learn (\LatOnnxOK{} of \LatOnnxN{} exports, decision agreement at least \LatOnnxAgreeMin). A call that does nothing has a 99th percentile of \LatFloorPnn{}\,ms, too little to distort the comparisons.

Implementation matters more than architecture (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}). For LR, the DataFrame path takes \LatLRdfPfifty{}\,ms at the median against \LatLRarrPfifty{}\,ms for an array, and input validation accounts for the difference. RF, asked to predict one row through its default thread pool, takes \LatRFthreadsPfifty{}\,ms at the median, compared with \LatRFarrPfifty{}\,ms on one thread and \LatRFonnxPfifty{}\,ms in ONNX Runtime. On the radio layer the 99th percentile under ONNX Runtime lies between \LatOnnxPnnMin{} and \LatOnnxPnnMax{}\,ms for all architectures, while scikit-learn with arrays reaches up to \LatArrPnnMax{}\,ms. Rank architectures by latency and you mostly rank implementations. Numbers from other hosts or languages~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime} tell us nothing about the architectures for the same reason. For context only, Obiuwevwi et al. report inference in the microsecond range for logistic regression and a small MLP inside an OpenAirInterface and FlexRIC near-real-time RIC~\cite{obiuwevwi2026realtime}, within an order of magnitude of our ONNX Runtime figures.

A window-level detector aggregates a window and then runs inference. Aggregating one 16-record window takes \LatAggPnn{}\,ms at the 99th percentile, and across the architectures with a verified export the upper bound of the 99th percentile of aggregation plus ONNX inference is at most \LatEtoEPnnHiMax{}\,ms. On this host, those two terms alone would fit any budget in the specified range. We repeated the radio measurement on the same host and recorded every call for Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}. The medians came out at \LatRepMedRatioMin{} to \LatRepMedRatioMax{} times those in Table~\ref{tab:latency} (median ratio \LatRepMedRatio). Without CPU isolation, per-call latency depends on the state of the host as well as on the model. The upper 95\% bound of the 99th percentile of aggregation plus ONNX inference nonetheless stayed at or below \LatRepEtoEPnnHiMax\,ms in the repeat.

Table~\ref{tab:ric} adds the remaining terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:latency}, measured in the FlexRIC loop of Section~\ref{sec:setup}. The six models ran with a report every 10\,ms (\RicNten{} reports each after 200 warm-up reports), and LR and RF also with a report every 1\,ms (\RicNone{} reports each after 1,000 warm-up reports). Reports carried \RicUEs{} UEs on average, and the xApp scored all of them. At the 10\,ms period, a report takes \RicIndTenPfifty{}\,ms at the median to get from the E2 node to the xApp, and the control round trip takes \RicActTenPfifty{}\,ms. Inference in the same loop needs at most \RicInfTenPnnMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile. For every model, delivery and control account for at least \RicTransportShareMin\% of the mean loop time. Once a RIC is in the path, the choice of model barely matters. The full loop, from report to acknowledged control message, takes \RicLoopTenPnnMin{} to \RicLoopTenPnnMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile, with an upper 95\% bound of at most \RicLoopTenPnnHiMax{}\,ms. That meets the smallest specified budget of 10\,ms even at the 99.9th percentile (at most \RicLoopTenPnnnMax{}\,ms). The largest single value we saw at this period was \RicLoopTenMax{}\,ms.

Oddly, the loop ran faster at the 1\,ms period. Its 99th percentile fell to \RicLoopOnePnnMin{} to \RicLoopOnePnnMax{}\,ms, the xApp never queued more than \RicQueueMax{} reports, and no run dropped a report. Every term got faster, inference included (at most \RicInfOnePnnMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile). We did not control CPU power management, and a host that idles between sparse reports and pays to wake up would produce exactly this pattern. Keep in mind that the loop runs on one host, with an emulated node and loopback SCTP. It bounds the processing cost of the RIC software path. The transport delay of a real deployment is not in it.

For a flow-level detector the expensive part is feature construction, not inference. On the three DDoS captures of \DA{}, the vectorized exporter produces flow records at \LatExtrMin{} to \LatExtrMax{}\,ms per flow, \LatExtrSpeedMin{} to \LatExtrSpeedMax{} times faster than the per-packet reference implementation on the same captures (\LatExtrRefMin{} to \LatExtrRefMax{}\,ms per flow), and the cost per flow grows with the number of packets in the flow. On the \LatEqCaptures{} captures compared record by record, the two exporters agree exactly in packets and bytes on all \LatEqKeys{} shared IPv4 flow keys. The reference implementation also emits \LatRefOnlyMin{} and \LatRefOnlyMax{} IPv6 link-local keys, which the vectorized exporter skips because it parses IPv4 only. Mapping one exported record onto the shared space with our pandas code costs another \LatMapPfifty{}\,ms at the median, whereas ONNX inference on flow features needs at most \LatNetOnnxPnnMax{}\,ms at the 99th percentile. These numbers describe a Python pipeline on one host. They place the cost in feature construction but cannot tell us what a native exporter at the user plane would cost.
